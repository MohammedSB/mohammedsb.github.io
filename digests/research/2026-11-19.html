1. Rethinking Contrastive Targets in Radiology Language-Image Pretraining

Yunhe Gao, Ashwin Kumar, Jiaming Liu, Chong Wang et al. (11 authors)  ·  2611.24016

Vision-Language ModelsContrastive LearningRadiology

TL;DR: This paper examines why standard CLIP-style image-report contrastive pretraining fails in radiology and changes how the contrastive targets are defined.

Problem: Radiology CLIP pretraining pairs each image with one report and treats every other report in the batch as a negative. This causes two failures that existing contrastive learning theory does not address: zero-shot performance peaks and then falls while training loss keeps dropping, and training becomes unstable.

Method: The authors analyze the one-positive, all-others-negative target in radiology data, where many reports in a batch describe clinically similar findings, so many 'negatives' are not really negative. They then propose revised contrastive targets that account for this overlap between reports, rather than relying on hard one-hot instance matching.

Results: The abstract shown is cut off. It identifies the zero-shot degradation and instability failures, and the revised targets are presented as the fix for both.

Why it matters: This goes straight at CLIP-style pretraining for radiology, the core of your vision-language foundation model work. It is a likely fix for false negatives and late-training zero-shot collapse in chest X-ray and report models.

2. ProtoBridge-CLIP: Observation-Masked Finding Prototypes for Medical Image--Report Representation Learning

Jinrui Wu, Yun Peng  ·  2611.24002

Medical Image-Report AlignmentVision-Language ModelsRadiology Reports

TL;DR: ProtoBridge-CLIP combines global image-report contrastive learning with finding-level prototypes that separate affirmed, negated, and unmentioned findings.

Problem: Radiology reports separate affirmed findings, explicitly negated findings, and concepts that are never mentioned. Global image-report contrastive learning squeezes all three evidence states into one study-level target and loses fine-grained clinical meaning.

Method: The model keeps symmetric global image-report alignment. It adds observation-masked finding supervision, where only findings the report actually observes (affirmed or negated) supervise learned finding prototypes. A soft relation objective over affirmed and negated findings shapes the shared embedding space.

Results: The abstract shown is cut off. The method is presented as producing representations that respect negation and missing labels, intended to improve finding-level transfer over plain global CLIP alignment.

Why it matters: Handling negation and uncertainty is a known weak point in radiology vision-language pretraining and in report evaluation metrics. This finding-aware design ties directly into both your CLIP-style and report-generation work.

3. NativeSeg: Native Vision Language Segmentation of 3D Computed Tomography via Spatial Visual Tokens and Verified Local Zoom

Chengqian Ma, Gaole Dai, Zijie Meng, Weixuan Jin et al. (8 authors)  ·  2611.24443

Referring Segmentation3D CTMedical Vision-Language Models

TL;DR: NativeSeg makes a medical VLM segment 3D CT directly from text prompts, using spatial visual tokens and a verified local zoom step instead of handing masks off to an external SAM decoder.

Problem: Text-prompted 3D CT segmentation needs both whole-slice context and precise boundaries, and over 80% of slices for a prompted organ do not contain the target. Existing medical VLMs compress their reasoning into a single 1D [SEG] vector and pass it to an external segmenter, which loses spatial detail.

Method: NativeSeg segments natively inside the VLM using spatial visual tokens that keep the layout of the slice. A verified local zoom mechanism first checks whether the target is present, then refines boundaries at higher resolution, which suppresses false positives on slices without the target.

Results: The abstract shown is cut off. It is presented as more precise and less prone to hallucinated masks than [SEG]-token VLM segmenters on text-prompted CT segmentation.

Why it matters: This is referring segmentation for 3D medical imaging built on a vision-language backbone, which matches your interests in referring segmentation and medical vision-language models.

1. ReCoCo: From Patches to Compositions for Visual Representation Learning

Juan Gutiérrez, Saman Zadtootaghaj, José Luis Blanco-Murillo, Sara Atito  ·  2611.24806

Self-Supervised LearningDense Representation LearningVision Transformers

TL;DR: ReCoCo learns dense visual features by enforcing consistency between compositions of patches, rather than matching single patches or pre-identified regions across augmented views.

Problem: Consistency between augmented views gives strong image-level self-supervision but is hard to extend to local content. Matching individual patches across views, or first identifying coherent regions, introduces uncertainty about which local pieces actually correspond.

Method: ReCoCo moves from patch-level to composition-level targets. It builds compositions of patches and enforces cross-view consistency on them, avoiding brittle one-to-one patch correspondence and explicit region discovery.

Results: The abstract shown is cut off. The method is positioned as giving stronger dense features than patch-matching and region-based self-supervised baselines on dense downstream tasks.

Why it matters: Dense self-supervised features underpin label-efficient medical segmentation and grounding, so this objective may carry over to pretraining on chest X-ray and other medical images.

2. Counterfactual World Models: JEPAs That Reason About "What If"

Randall Balestriero, Yann LeCun, Kerda Varaku  ·  2611.24778

World ModelsJEPACounterfactual Reasoning

TL;DR: The authors prove that world models trained only on factual transitions cannot answer counterfactual 'what if I had acted differently' questions under hidden disturbances, and propose JEPAs that can.

Problem: World models act in partially observed environments where hidden disturbances change the outcome of an action. Answering what would have happened under a different action with the same disturbance is what separates a bad decision from bad luck, and standard world models are not built for it.

Method: The authors give a formal impossibility result for world models trained on factual transitions alone. They then build a counterfactual JEPA (Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture) that infers the latent disturbance and keeps it fixed while varying the action.

Results: The main result is the proof that factual-only training cannot identify counterfactuals. The counterfactual JEPA is presented as recovering these answers where standard world models fail.

Why it matters: This is a principled step for JEPA-style self-supervised predictive models (LeCun et al.). The counterfactual reasoning ideas could matter for treatment-effect style questions in clinical decision models.

3. The Elephant in the Question: Unified Models Picture the Concepts Behind Their Answers

Tobias Braun, Marcus Rohrbach, Lorenzo Cavallaro  ·  2611.24793

Unified Multimodal ModelsInterpretabilityVision-Language Models

TL;DR: The V-lens uses the image generator inside a unified multimodal model to visualize what a chosen token's hidden state represents, showing the concepts behind the model's answers.

Problem: Inspecting the intermediate reasoning of multimodal models is hard. Text-based probes do not show what concepts the model actually forms while it processes a question.

Method: Unified models combine understanding and generation in one architecture, and their image generators can read representations formed while processing text. The V-lens feeds a chosen token's hidden state to the generator and renders it as an image.

Results: The rendered images show that models picture concepts implied by a question but never stated in it (the 'elephant in the question'). This gives an interpretability tool for intermediate reasoning steps.

Why it matters: This new interpretability method for vision-language foundation models could be adapted to check what medical vision-language models picture internally before they generate reports.
