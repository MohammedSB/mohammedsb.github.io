1. Where Does Free-Text 3D Grounding Fail? An Error Analysis of Report-Sentence Segmentation in Chest CT

Abdullah Arif Sheikh, Kamalaker Dadi, Raju S. Bapi  ·  2611.33958

Referring SegmentationChest CTRadiology Report Grounding

TL;DR: An error analysis of VoxTell, a free-text 3D grounding model, on all 381 ReXGroundingCT validation findings shows where segmentation from radiology-report sentences succeeds and where it fails.

Problem: Segmenting the CT region that one report sentence describes could link reports to images at scale. Aggregate scores do not show where current models go wrong.

Method: The authors run the publicly released VoxTell model on all 381 findings in the 200-scan ReXGroundingCT validation set. They then sort its failures by type, such as wrong lesion, wrong side, or extent and boundary errors, to find out what actually limits grounding quality.

Results: VoxTell grounds 53.5% of findings. Its failures are rarely about picking the wrong lesion or the wrong side, which suggests that the remaining errors come mostly from other factors such as extent, size, or the type of finding.

Why it matters: This applies referring segmentation directly to radiology report text, which sits where your report-generation and referring-segmentation interests meet. Its error taxonomy could guide grounding-based metrics for evaluating generated reports.

2. Sage-Med: Scaling Medical Multimodal Intelligence

Sage-Med Team, :, Ming Hu, Cheng Tang et al. (109 authors)  ·  2611.34575

Medical Multimodal LLMsScaling LawsFoundation Models

TL;DR: Sage-Med is a family of medical multimodal LLMs built to find scaling recipes that actually improve multimodal perception, not only text ability.

Problem: In the authors' systematic evaluation, existing medical MLLMs often trail frontier general-purpose MLLMs on medical tasks. Making these models bigger mostly improves text ability rather than multimodal perception, and turning on reasoning does not reliably help.

Method: The team runs a large benchmark of medical and general MLLMs to diagnose these scaling failures. They then build Sage-Med around data and training choices aimed at scaling medical visual perception together with language ability.

Results: Sage-Med is reported to narrow or close the gap with frontier general MLLMs across a broad set of medical imaging tasks. Full numbers are in the paper.

Why it matters: It gives direct evidence on whether domain-specific medical VLMs are still worth building compared with general frontier models. That matters for anyone choosing backbones for radiology VLM work.

3. Can Its Own Misses Teach an MLLM to Find Small Brain Metastases?

Yixuan Duan, Kexin Geng, Hanwen Xu, Wei Qiu  ·  2611.33836

Brain MRISmall Lesion DetectionMedical MLLMs

TL;DR: The paper asks whether an MLLM can get better at localizing small brain metastases by training on the lesions it previously missed.

Problem: MLLMs are increasingly used as general medical image readers but are poor at finding small abnormalities. At 384 px a 0.1 mL metastasis covers less than a quarter of one visual token, and fine-tuning on a single dataset transfers poorly.

Method: The authors measure how frontier MLLMs detect lesions of different sizes. They then propose a self-improvement scheme in which the model's own misses (false negatives) become targeted training signal for small-lesion localization.

Results: At one false positive per scan, frontier MLLMs find fewer than 30% of 0.1–1 mL lesions. The miss-driven training is reported to improve small-lesion detection and cross-dataset transfer.

Why it matters: Token-resolution limits on small findings affect chest X-ray and CT VLMs just as much. The failure-driven training recipe could carry over to detecting subtle radiology findings.

Top General AI Papers — Full Summary

1. MulCLIP: A Multi-level Alignment Framework for Enhancing Fine-grained Long-context CLIP

Chau Truong, Hieu Ta Quang, Zhenzhen Liu, Dung D. Le  ·  2611.34083

Vision-Language ModelsCLIPLong-Text Alignment

TL;DR: MulCLIP is an end-to-end, multi-level alignment framework that lets CLIP handle long, detailed captions without costly region proposals.

Problem: CLIP-style models are trained on short captions and struggle with long, fine-grained descriptions. Recent fixes align region proposals with individual sentences, which is expensive to deploy.

Method: MulCLIP uses the natural structure of long text (the whole caption, its sentences, and finer phrases) and aligns each level with matching visual granularity in one end-to-end model. No external region-proposal stage is needed.

Results: It is reported to improve long-text and fine-grained retrieval over long-context CLIP baselines while costing less to deploy than region-based methods.

Why it matters: Radiology reports are exactly the long, multi-sentence, finding-level text that breaks standard CLIP. Multi-level sentence and phrase alignment of this kind fits report-image pretraining for chest X-rays well.

2. Tune for Retention, Not Just Gain: SFT Forgets Far Less than You Think

Nirmit Joshi, Nathan Srebro, Eran Malach  ·  2611.34579

Fine-TuningCatastrophic ForgettingLLM Training

TL;DR: Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) causes far less catastrophic forgetting than widely believed once hyperparameters are tuned for retention as well as task gain.

Problem: SFT is often said to cause severe forgetting, and recent work blames its off-policy nature and recommends on-policy learning instead.

Method: The authors re-run comparisons while tuning SFT hyperparameters, such as learning rate and training length, to preserve earlier capabilities rather than only to maximize gains on the new task. They then compare forgetting against on-policy alternatives.

Results: With retention-aware tuning, SFT keeps most prior capabilities and stays competitive with on-policy methods, which undercuts the claim that it is inherently prone to forgetting.

Why it matters: It is practical guidance for adapting general VLMs or LLMs to medical data without losing their general skills. Tuning for retention may matter more than switching to RL-style training.

3. Can image generation help visual reasoning? An empirical study

Xueyang Yu, Yifan Jing, Taiming Lu, Linrong Cai et al. (8 authors)  ·  2611.34935

Multimodal ReasoningImage GenerationVision-Language Models

TL;DR: Across 13 pairs of vision-language model and image generator, letting the model generate a helper image before answering lowers visual question-answering accuracy.

Problem: Models that 'think in images' are a popular idea, but whether a generated helper image actually improves visual reasoning has not been tested carefully.

Method: In a controlled protocol, a VLM describes the helper image it needs, a generator produces it, and the VLM answers using the original and generated images together. The setup is repeated across many VLM–generator combinations and benchmarks.

Results: Generation lowers mean accuracy by 3.7 percentage points, with declines on nine of the ten settings reported.

Why it matters: It is a useful caution before adding generative 'visual chain-of-thought' steps to medical VQA or report pipelines. Generated images can add noise rather than evidence.
