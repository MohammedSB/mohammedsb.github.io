1. SMILE: A Structured Multi-Agent Clinical Reasoning for Liver Ultrasound Understanding

Haris Ghafoor, Minjee Seo, Seonaeng cho, Keungmo Yang et al. (6 authors)  ·  2611.02581

Radiology Report GenerationMultimodal ModelsUltrasound

TL;DR: A structured multi-agent clinical reasoning framework for automated liver ultrasound report generation that separates perception, diagnosis, and reporting into specialized agents.

Problem: Current vision-language models for liver ultrasound report generation rely on a single reasoning pathway that maps ultrasound appearance directly to text, failing to capture the structured clinical reasoning process that radiologists use. Additionally, the inherent vision-language modality gap makes it difficult for models to accurately interpret disease-specific ultrasound patterns.

Method: SMILE proposes a multi-agent framework with specialized roles: a perception agent extracts anatomical findings, a diagnosis agent performs clinical reasoning about disease states, and a reporting agent generates structured reports. The framework uses a structured reasoning protocol that enforces clinically meaningful intermediate steps and integrates domain-specific knowledge about liver pathology.

Results: SMILE outperforms existing generative VLMs on liver ultrasound report generation, producing more clinically accurate and structured descriptions of anatomical and disease-related findings. The multi-agent decomposition leads to improved performance on both finding detection and report coherence metrics.

Why it matters: Directly relevant to the reader's interests in radiology report generation and vision-language models for medical imaging; demonstrates a novel multi-agent approach to structured clinical report generation that could extend to chest X-ray or other modalities.

2. Findings-aware Language-Image Pretraining for CT Volume Interpretation

Takuro Shimaya, Junya Inoh, Masahiro Saiko  ·  2611.02327

Medical ImagingVision-Language ModelsCT Analysis

TL;DR: A findings-aware language-image pretraining method for CT volumes that aligns visual representations with specific radiology report findings rather than entire reports.

Problem: Existing CT vision-language foundation models align whole CT volumes with entire radiology reports, which conflates diverse clinical findings and limits the model's ability to localize and distinguish specific pathological features. This coarse alignment makes it difficult to learn fine-grained clinical representations needed for various downstream tasks.

Method: The proposed approach decomposes radiology reports into individual findings and performs findings-aware contrastive pretraining that aligns CT volume representations with specific finding-level text descriptions. The framework uses a region-finding correspondence mechanism to create spatially grounded representations for each clinical observation extracted from reports.

Results: The findings-aware pretraining produces superior feature representations for multiple downstream CT interpretation tasks compared to report-level alignment baselines, showing improved performance on classification, segmentation, and retrieval tasks across diverse CT datasets.

Why it matters: Highly relevant to the reader's work on CLIP-style vision-language foundation models and self-supervised learning for medical imaging; the findings-level alignment approach directly addresses limitations of current medical VLPs like MedCLIP and BioViL.

3. Multidimensional Suicide Risk Assessment with Machine Learning using Electronic Health Records

Nathan Scollar, Kemal Davaslioglu, Nathan Conger, Sastry Kompella et al. (6 authors)  ·  2611.03357

Electronic Health RecordsClinical Risk PredictionMachine Learning

TL;DR: A multidimensional machine learning framework using MIMIC-IV EHR data to model and predict multiple suicide-related outcomes across clinical, behavioral, and social dimensions.

Problem: Suicide risk assessment in clinical practice relies on limited screening tools that fail to capture the multidimensional nature of risk, which spans clinical diagnoses, behavioral patterns, medication history, and social determinants. Electronic health records contain rich longitudinal data that could enable more comprehensive risk stratification but are rarely exploited jointly.

Method: The framework constructs multiple outcome variables from MIMIC-IV EHR data capturing different facets of suicide-related risk, then trains and evaluates a suite of machine learning models including gradient boosting and neural networks on structured clinical features. A multidimensional evaluation protocol assesses performance across the different outcome dimensions simultaneously.

Results: The approach demonstrates that different risk dimensions require different modeling strategies, and that jointly modeling multiple outcomes using EHR-derived features provides more comprehensive risk characterization than single-outcome models. Performance varies substantially across risk dimensions, revealing which aspects of suicidality are more predictable from structured clinical data.

Why it matters: Relevant to the reader's interest in AI for healthcare and clinical practice; demonstrates applied ML on real EHR data for a high-stakes clinical prediction problem, with implications for how AI tools can augment clinical decision-making.

1. Library of Layers (LoL): Adaptively Ordering Transformer Layers at Runtime

Abdellah Rahmani, Sean McLeish, Ang Li, Sanae Lotfi et al. (6 authors)  ·  2611.03526

Foundation ModelsDynamic InferenceTransformer Architecture

TL;DR: Library of Layers (LoL) is a novel architecture that dynamically reorders transformer layers at inference time rather than executing them in a fixed order, enabling more flexible computation.

Problem: All existing transformer-based language models execute their layers in a fixed predetermined order, which limits their ability to adapt computation to the specific requirements of different inputs or tasks. This static execution order wastes capacity on problems that could be solved with a different arrangement of the same computational primitives.

Method: LoL introduces a learned routing mechanism that selects and orders a subset of reusable layers at runtime for each input, analogous to how programmers reuse the same computational primitives in different orders. Each layer is trained to be reusable across different positions in the execution sequence, and a lightweight controller determines the optimal ordering and selection at inference.

Results: LoL achieves competitive or better performance than fixed-order transformers while enabling dynamic computation allocation, with the layer ordering adapting meaningfully to different input types. The architecture demonstrates that dynamic layer ordering is a viable and beneficial design choice for language models.

Why it matters: Directly relevant to the reader's interest in vision-language foundation models and transformer architectures; this architectural innovation could inform the design of more efficient and adaptive medical imaging models where different inputs (e.g., normal vs. pathological scans) may benefit from different computational pathways.

2. Balancing Representations by Identifying and Generating Under-represented Data

Filipe Laitenberger, Hirokatsu Kataoka, Yuki Asano, Cees Snoek et al. (5 authors)  ·  2611.02952

Self-Supervised LearningData AugmentationLong-Tail Learning

TL;DR: BRIDGE identifies under-represented modes in self-supervised learning corpora and uses generative models to synthesize additional training data for those regions, improving representation balance.

Problem: Self-supervised learning inherits the coverage biases of its training data; when source datasets are long-tailed, representations become dense around frequent modes while under-represented concepts receive weak coverage. Existing methods for long-tailed SSL only reweight existing images without adding new coverage.

Method: BRIDGE uses clustering of pretrained visual representations to identify sparse regions in the data distribution, then employs generative models to synthesize new training images targeted at those under-represented regions. The synthesized images are incorporated into SSL training without requiring any labels, effectively flattening the data distribution.

Results: BRIDGE improves downstream task performance on multiple benchmarks, particularly for rare classes, by producing more balanced visual representations. The approach outperforms reweighting-only baselines and demonstrates that generative data augmentation can meaningfully improve SSL when applied to identified coverage gaps.

Why it matters: Highly relevant to the reader's focus on self-supervised learning and multimodal learning for medical imaging, where data imbalance is pervasive (e.g., rare pathologies); the approach of using generative models to fill representation gaps is directly applicable to medical imaging datasets.

3. Unimodal Augmentation-based SSL Objectives Induce Cross-Modal Alignment

Zohar Mashiah, Nir Ben-Ari, Ronen Talmon, Uri Shaham  ·  2611.02917

Self-Supervised LearningMultimodal AlignmentRepresentation Learning

TL;DR: This paper proves theoretically that unimodal augmentation-based self-supervised objectives implicitly induce cross-modal alignment between independently trained models, explaining observed emergent alignment phenomena.

Problem: Recent works have observed that independently trained unimodal models (e.g., vision-only and language-only) exhibit implicit cross-modal alignment, but the mechanism behind this emergent phenomenon is not understood. This limits our ability to deliberately engineer or improve cross-modal representations.

Method: The authors develop a theoretical framework showing that low unimodal losses under augmentation-based SSL objectives mathematically force high cross-modal alignment, while high unimodal losses allow misalignment. The key insight is that augmentation invariances shared across modalities create implicit coupling between independently trained models, which they formalize and prove under mild assumptions.

Results: Experiments across multiple modality pairs and SSL objectives confirm the theoretical predictions: models with lower within-modality SSL losses consistently exhibit stronger cross-modal alignment. The theory also predicts the conditions under which this alignment fails, enabling principled design of SSL objectives.

Why it matters: Directly relevant to the reader's interests in self-supervised learning and CLIP-style vision-language models; provides theoretical grounding for why unimodal pretraining transfers to multimodal settings, with implications for designing better medical vision-language pretraining strategies.
