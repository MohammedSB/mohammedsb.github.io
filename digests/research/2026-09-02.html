1. RadMatch: Auditable Radiology Report Evaluation via Finding-Level Matching

Charles Corbière, Léo Machado, Aubin Charley, Baptiste Callard et al. (6 authors)  ·  2609.01470

Radiology Report GenerationEvaluation MetricsLLM-as-a-Judge

TL;DR: RadMatch is an LLM-based radiology report metric that scores generated reports by explicitly matching individual findings between candidate and reference, yielding an auditable, decomposable score instead of one opaque number.

Problem: LLM-based metrics currently correlate best with radiologist judgment but emit a single opaque score that clinicians and model developers cannot inspect, attribute, or debug. This blocks their use as trustworthy evaluation or as targeted feedback for report-generation systems.

Method: RadMatch is a multi-stage LLM pipeline that first decomposes reports into discrete findings, then aligns candidate findings to reference findings, and finally classifies each alignment (match, omission, hallucination, or disagreement in severity/location). The resulting finding-level match structure is aggregated into a score whose every component can be traced back to a specific clinical statement.

Results: The authors report that finding-level matching preserves or improves correlation with radiologist assessment relative to existing single-score LLM metrics, while additionally exposing which specific findings drive each score.

Why it matters: This sits directly on the reader's work on evaluation metrics for generative radiology reports: it is a concrete, auditable alternative to RadGraph-F1/GREEN-style scores and an immediately usable diagnostic tool for report-generation error analysis.

2. A multicenter benchmark and clinically structured metric for coronary CTA report generation

Zhiyu Ye, Yue Sun, Limiao Zou, Cheng Xu et al. (10 authors)  ·  2609.00909

Radiology Report Generation3D Vision-Language ModelsClinical Benchmark

TL;DR: A four-centre coronary CTA benchmark of 3,021 series with 818 patient–report pairs, plus a clinically structured metric (CSM_CCTA), used to evaluate seven open-source 3D vision-language models on report generation.

Problem: Automated report generation for coronary CT angiography lacks standardized multicentre evaluation data and metrics that reflect the structured, per-vessel clinical content radiologists actually care about. Generic NLG scores conflate fluency with diagnostic correctness.

Method: The authors assemble a multicentre CCTA benchmark spanning four hospitals with paired patient reports, then define CSM_CCTA, a clinically structured metric that decomposes report quality along clinically meaningful axes (e.g. vessel-level stenosis and plaque descriptors) rather than surface n-gram overlap. Seven open-source 3D vision-language models are benchmarked under this protocol.

Results: The evaluation exposes substantial gaps between current open-source 3D VLMs and clinically acceptable CCTA reporting, and shows where structured scoring diverges from conventional NLG metrics.

Why it matters: It extends the report-generation-plus-clinical-metric agenda from chest X-ray to volumetric cardiac imaging, offering both a metric design the reader can compare against and a rare multicentre 3D VLM baseline suite.

3. CMRVision: A Foundation Model for Cardiac MR Image Analysis

Athira J. Jacob, Puneet Sharma, Daniel Rueckert  ·  2609.01308

Self-Supervised LearningFoundation ModelsCardiac MRI

TL;DR: CMRVision is a cardiac-MR-specific vision foundation model pretrained with DINOv3-style self-supervision on ~36 million multi-centre, multi-sequence 2D CMR images.

Problem: Cardiac MR analysis spans many sequences and views (cine, LGE, mapping) with complementary anatomical, functional and tissue information, but task- and sequence-specific models must be retrained for each downstream problem and generalize poorly across centres.

Method: The authors curate a large multi-centre, multi-sequence CMR corpus and pretrain a 2D vision backbone with DINOv3-style self-distillation objectives, deliberately avoiding annotation dependence. The resulting representations are then evaluated by transferring to a range of downstream CMR tasks across anatomy, function and tissue characterization.

Results: CMRVision's self-supervised features transfer across multiple CMR downstream tasks and sequences, with reported gains over generic and smaller-scale pretrained baselines.

Why it matters: A clean, large-scale case study of DINO-family self-supervision applied to a non-CXR medical modality — directly informative for the reader's self-supervised medical imaging work and for judging how modality-specific pretraining corpora should be assembled.

1. Revisiting Cross-View Completion: Self-Supervised Pre-Training via Reconstruction Error Comparison

Thibaut Loiseau, Guillaume Bourmaud, Vincent Lepetit  ·  2609.01530

Self-Supervised LearningCross-View Completion3D Vision

TL;DR: Gekko turns the weak monocular signal in non-co-visible regions of cross-view completion into an explicit training target by comparing reconstruction errors with and without the reference view.

Problem: Cross-view completion pretraining (à la CroCo) learns strong 3D features from co-visible image regions, but for patches not visible in the reference view the reference contributes nothing, so the objective silently degenerates into monocular reconstruction there.

Method: Gekko reframes the objective around the *relative* improvement in reconstruction that the reference view provides for each patch, i.e. comparing cross-view reconstruction error against a reference-free baseline. This comparison yields a per-region signal that identifies genuine co-visibility and concentrates learning on patches where cross-view information actually matters.

Results: The reformulated objective improves the learned representations for downstream 3D vision tasks relative to standard cross-view completion pretraining.

Why it matters: A neat instance of diagnosing a degenerate self-supervised objective and converting the failure mode into supervision — the same reasoning pattern applies to masked/contrastive pretraining on paired or multi-view medical images.

2. Uncovering Understanding-Generation Synergy in Native Unified Multimodal Models: From Representation, Task to System

Penghao Wu, Haiwen Diao, Weichen Fan, Lewei Lu et al. (6 authors)  ·  2609.01607

Unified Multimodal ModelsVisual Understanding And GenerationRepresentation Analysis

TL;DR: A controlled study of whether visual understanding and generation actually help each other inside native unified multimodal models, analyzed at the representation, task, and system levels.

Problem: Unified multimodal models perform understanding and generation in one model, but architectural unification does not imply learning synergy: the two objectives may reinforce, compete for capacity, or simply coexist. Existing work rarely separates these possibilities.

Method: The authors build a structurally native unified setting without inherited pretraining shortcuts and probe the understanding–generation relationship at three levels: shared representations, cross-task transfer, and full-system behavior. Controlled ablations isolate when one objective's gradient helps or harms the other.

Results: The analysis identifies regimes where synergy is genuine versus where the two objectives merely coexist or contend for capacity, giving concrete conditions under which unification pays off.

Why it matters: Directly relevant for anyone considering joint image-understanding + image/report-generation models: it clarifies whether adding generation to an understanding model should be expected to improve representations at all.

3. When Modality Gap Reduction Fails: Prediction-Level Hubness in CLIP

Shota Sato, Hajime Kiyama, Tosho Hirasawa, Mamoru Komachi  ·  2609.01103

CLIPModality GapZero-Shot Classification

TL;DR: Closing CLIP's image–text modality gap does not reliably improve zero-shot accuracy, because errors are driven by prediction-level hubness in the class-prototype decision structure rather than by average cross-modal distance.

Problem: The field widely assumes that shrinking the average gap between CLIP image and text embeddings improves cross-modal alignment and downstream performance, yet reported accuracy gains are inconsistent.

Method: The authors analyze zero-shot classification as prototype selection and measure hubness at the level of predictions — whether a few class-text prototypes dominate as nearest neighbors for many images. They then relate modality-gap reduction interventions to changes in this prediction-level hub structure rather than to embedding-distance statistics.

Results: Reducing the mean modality gap can leave or even worsen prediction-level hubness, explaining why gap-closing methods fail to yield consistent zero-shot accuracy improvements.

Why it matters: Squarely on the reader's CLIP-style foundation model interests: it argues the standard modality-gap diagnostic is the wrong objective and offers a better-targeted failure metric for CLIP-based medical zero-shot pipelines.
