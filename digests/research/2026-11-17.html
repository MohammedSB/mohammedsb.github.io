1. MedJev: A System One Vision-Language Decision Model for Fast and Effective Medical Image Understanding

Fan Zhang, Hao Wu, Jinyang Wu, Hongyu Qu et al. (8 authors)  ·  2611.20992

Medical VQAVision-Language ModelsEfficient Inference

TL;DR: MedJev is a 'system one' medical vision-language decision model. It gives an answer directly in one step instead of generating reasoning and answer tokens one at a time.

Problem: Medical VLMs usually generate reasoning and answer tokens one by one. Inference cost then grows with response length, and the answer has to be parsed out of free-form text, which adds latency and parsing errors.

Method: MedJev treats medical image understanding as a direct decision. It returns a typed answer over the declared options in a single forward pass rather than generating text. This removes the need for chain-of-thought tokens and post-hoc answer extraction.

Results: The abstract claims fast and effective medical image understanding with lower latency and fewer decision errors than autoregressive VLM baselines. The specific numbers were cut off in the provided text.

Why it matters: This applies directly to deploying VLMs for chest X-ray and radiology question answering and classification, where latency and reliable answers matter. It also offers a different design from generation-based medical VLMs that is worth comparing with your own CLIP-style and report-generation work.

2. FM-Recon: Exploring Foundation Model Features for Accelerated Cardiac Cine MRI Reconstruction

Donghang Lyu, Marius Staring, Ruochen Gao, Jochen Keupp et al. (6 authors)  ·  2611.21290

MRI ReconstructionFoundation ModelsCardiac Imaging

TL;DR: FM-Recon tests whether features from pretrained foundation models help unrolled networks reconstruct accelerated cardiac cine MRI, including inputs with artifacts.

Problem: Foundation models extract strong features across many medical imaging tasks, but their value for MRI reconstruction is largely unexplored. This is especially true inside unrolled reconstruction networks and when inputs are corrupted by artifacts.

Method: The authors add features from pretrained foundation models to unrolled reconstruction networks for accelerated cardiac cine MRI. They then study both whether and how these features help, under clean and artifact-corrupted undersampled inputs.

Results: The paper reports that foundation-model features can benefit accelerated cine MRI reconstruction. The detailed gains were cut off in the provided abstract.

Why it matters: This shows self-supervised foundation-model representations being used beyond recognition tasks, in physics-based reconstruction. That is relevant to your interest in how pretrained visual features transfer across medical imaging tasks.

3. When Do Histology Foundation Models Need Geometric Test-Time Augmentation?

Lars Chen, Valentino F. Foit, Dorry Segev, Michal Mankowski  ·  2611.21565

HistopathologyFoundation ModelsTest-Time Augmentation

TL;DR: This paper asks whether standard D4 rotation/reflection test-time augmentation is still needed for self-supervised histology foundation models, which may already be rotation-invariant.

Problem: Histology images have no canonical orientation, so averaging predictions over the 8 D4 rotations and reflections is a default in computational pathology. That multiplies inference cost by 8, and modern self-supervised foundation models may already be invariant enough to make it unnecessary.

Method: The authors run a systematic evaluation of geometric TTA across self-supervised histology foundation models. They study when pooling over the 8 transformed views actually changes predictions or performance, given each model's built-in rotational invariance.

Results: The paper gives guidance on when histology foundation models need D4 TTA and when it adds compute without benefit. Exact findings were cut off in the provided abstract.

Why it matters: This is a practical check on how invariant self-supervised medical foundation models really are. The same kind of evaluation could be applied to augmentation and invariance assumptions in chest X-ray foundation models.

Top General AI Papers — Full Summary

1. Learning What Pairs Miss for Higher-Order Multimodal Alignment

Kunyu Zhang, Shenghui Feng, Xiangjie Shi, Zeqing Wang et al. (5 authors)  ·  2611.21372

Contrastive LearningMultimodal AlignmentRepresentation Learning

TL;DR: Contrastive learning over three or more modalities in practice still acts like a 'bag of pairs'. This paper designs training that forces critics to learn genuinely higher-order alignment.

Problem: Contrastive objectives for more than two modalities score the whole tuple with multilinear products, volumes or fusion heads, which in principle can model the joint distribution. In practice their negatives break at least two of the three pairs, so a single pairwise check rejects them. When pairs can solve most samples, the higher-order term gets little learning signal.

Method: The authors analyze why tuple-level critics collapse to pairwise behavior. They then construct training signals, such as negatives that keep the pairs consistent but break the tuple, that can only be solved with higher-order information, so the joint term must learn what pairs miss.

Results: The paper reports that the method learns higher-order multimodal structure that standard pairwise and tuple critics miss. Quantitative results were cut off in the provided abstract.

Why it matters: This extends CLIP-style contrastive learning past two modalities. That is directly relevant if you combine images, reports and other clinical data, such as EHR or prior studies, in one contrastive framework.

2. How VLMs Plan What's Next

Rohit Gandikota, Arnab Sen Sharma, Adam Belfki, David Bau  ·  2611.21600

Vision-Language ModelsMechanistic InterpretabilityImage Captioning

TL;DR: This paper identifies the four-part internal mechanism a VLM uses to decide which object to describe next, and where to look before it looks.

Problem: When a VLM describes an image it names objects one at a time and rarely repeats itself. How the model plans this order internally is not understood.

Method: The authors trace the circuitry of the planning process. It includes 'binding heads' that match relevant text tokens against the image and resolve them into pointers to matched image regions, plus further components that track what has been mentioned and choose what comes next.

Results: The paper describes the mechanism in four main parts, explaining how VLMs order objects and avoid repetition during description.

Why it matters: Understanding how VLMs plan descriptive output bears directly on radiology report generation, where findings are omitted or repeated and the order of findings matters. It could also inform grounding analyses for referring segmentation.

3. SADL: On the Demand-Supply Geometry of Representation Collapse

Junhan Sun, Yigui Feng, Mingzhe Liu, Haoyan Xu et al. (10 authors)  ·  2611.21596

Self-Supervised LearningJEPARepresentation Collapse

TL;DR: SADL shows that representation collapse in JEPAs starts in the encoder, before the projector, and proposes a demand-supply geometric view to prevent it.

Problem: In Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures, a well-constrained projector output does not by itself prevent representation collapse. Regularizers placed after the projector can miss what is going wrong upstream.

Method: The authors show that from initialization the encoder representation is dominated by a component shared across inputs. A batch-normalized projector removes this component, so regularization after the projector cannot correct it. SADL frames collapse through a 'demand-supply' geometry and regularizes the encoder representation directly.

Results: The paper reports that SADL addresses collapse that post-projector regularization misses. Detailed benchmark numbers were cut off in the provided abstract.

Why it matters: JEPA-style and joint-embedding self-supervised learning is increasingly used for medical imaging pretraining. Knowing where collapse actually happens could improve self-supervised pretraining for chest X-rays and other modalities.
