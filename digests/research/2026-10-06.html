1. Anatomy-aware Fine-grained Multimodal Fusion for Laryngopharyngeal Cancer T-Staging Prediction Using CT and Radiology Report

Xingyue Zhao, Yanzhou Su, Fang Zhang, Zhanghexuan Ji et al. (15 authors)  ·  2610.06837

Multimodal FusionRadiology ReportsCancer Staging

TL;DR: An anatomy-aware model that combines CT images with radiology report text at a fine-grained level to predict laryngopharyngeal cancer T-stage without a biopsy.

Problem: T-staging of laryngopharyngeal cancer depends on invasive biopsy, and staging from CT alone is hard because tumor invasion patterns are complex. Existing computer-aided methods do not model the structural relationships between anatomical regions well, and they underuse the text in radiology reports.

Method: The framework explicitly models anatomical structure in the CT volume. It then fuses region-level image features with matching information from the paired radiology report, so that report statements align with specific anatomy rather than being merged at a global level. The fused representation is used to predict T-stage.

Results: The authors report better T-staging accuracy than image-only and coarse-fusion baselines. The abstract is truncated, so no exact numbers are given here.

Why it matters: This paper uses radiology reports as a supervisory and fusion signal aligned to anatomical regions, which is closely related to your work on image–report alignment and report generation. The region-level fusion design could also transfer to chest X-ray vision-language models.

2. Representation Disentanglement for Fair Chest X-Ray Diagnosis

Yujie Sun, Ruizhe Li, Xiaowu Sun  ·  2610.06041

Chest X-rayAlgorithmic FairnessContrastive Learning

TL;DR: A single-encoder chest X-ray classifier that separates demographic information from diagnostic features, reducing performance gaps across intersectional patient groups.

Problem: Deep chest X-ray models learn representations that carry demographic biases. This causes diagnostic performance to differ across intersectional groups, such as combinations of sex, race and age.

Method: The model uses decorrelation at two levels to reduce how much the learned representation depends on demographics. It adds prototype-guided contrastive learning across groups, which keeps natural variation within each disease class while aligning the same class across demographic groups. The authors also propose a new evaluation for intersectional fairness.

Results: The authors report smaller gaps between intersectional groups with diagnostic performance kept the same or improved, compared with fairness baselines on CXR benchmarks. Exact figures are not in the truncated abstract.

Why it matters: This is directly about chest X-ray AI. The contrastive and disentanglement methods could be added to CLIP-style CXR pretraining to make downstream diagnosis and report generation fairer.

3. BrainTRACE: Tracing Longitudinal, Multimodal, and Volumetric Evidence in Brain MRI Clinical Reasoning

Qizhen Lan, Mengchen Fan, Hang Zhang, Jingwei Duan et al. (8 authors)  ·  2610.06571

Brain MRIMedical VQA BenchmarkClinical Reasoning

TL;DR: BrainTRACE is a benchmark that tests whether models can reason about brain MRI the way radiologists do: across time points, across MRI sequences and in 3D, with assessments grounded in reports.

Problem: Existing medical VQA and 3D imaging benchmarks usually test brain MRI with single images or isolated tasks. They miss the radiologist's real workflow of comparing serial studies, combining sequences, localizing findings in a volume and writing a report-grounded assessment.

Method: The benchmark is built from longitudinal, multi-sequence, volumetric brain MRI studies paired with report-grounded questions. It traces which kinds of evidence (temporal, multimodal, volumetric) a model must use to answer, so failures can be attributed to a specific reasoning skill rather than to overall accuracy.

Results: The abstract is truncated, but it suggests current medical and general multimodal models have clear gaps on longitudinal and volumetric reasoning compared with single-image tasks.

Why it matters: Comparing a scan with prior studies and grounding findings in reports are core problems for radiology report generation and its evaluation. This benchmark's design is a useful template for CXR evaluation that accounts for prior studies.

1. Readout Blindness: VLM Scores Miss the Spatial Direction Their Frozen Encoders Retain

Guangyuan Li, Tianming Du, Yan Jiang, Bihan Wen et al. (5 authors)  ·  2610.06324

Vision-Language ModelsCLIPSpatial Reasoning

TL;DR: CLIP-style models fail at directional spatial relations such as 'left of' because of how their scores are computed, not because their frozen encoders lack the information.

Problem: CLIP-like models perform near chance on directed spatial relations, such as whether one object is to the left of another. It is unclear whether this is a limitation of the encoders or of how the encoders are read out.

Method: The authors name the failure 'readout blindness'. They show theoretically that when a scoring rule treats subject and object symmetrically, the direction cancels out regardless of what the encoder represents. They then probe frozen image and text encoders and find that the encoders still contain spatial direction, and they explore readouts that recover it.

Results: Frozen CLIP encoders contain recoverable directional spatial information that standard similarity scores discard. Readouts that respect direction improve performance on spatial-relation tasks.

Why it matters: Your work relies on CLIP-style foundation models. Laterality and spatial relations (left versus right lung, position relative to an anatomical landmark) are critical in radiology reports and referring segmentation, and this paper explains why contrastive scores miss them and how to recover that information.

2. From Pixels, Without Pre-training: Joint Generative and Self-Supervised Representation Learning in One Model

Vicente Balmaseda, Ching-Long Lin, Tianbao Yang  ·  2610.05711

Self-Supervised LearningDiffusion ModelsRepresentation Learning

TL;DR: A single model, trained from pixels with no labels or pretrained encoders, learns both to generate images and to produce strong self-supervised representations.

Problem: Strong image generators usually need class labels, alignment to frozen pretrained encoders (as in REPA) or separately trained autoencoders. That makes them depend on supervision or on pretraining in the target domain, which is a real limitation in domains like medical imaging.

Method: The paper trains a generative objective and a self-supervised representation objective jointly in one model, working directly on pixels without external pretraining. The two objectives are designed to help each other, so the representation guides generation and the generation task shapes the features.

Results: The authors report competitive generation quality and useful learned representations without labels or any pretrained component. Exact numbers are not in the truncated abstract.

Why it matters: In medical imaging, natural-image pretrained encoders transfer poorly and labels are scarce. A self-contained generative and SSL recipe could be used directly for CXR pretraining and synthetic data generation.

3. Base Models Can Reason By Taking a Cue From Training Data

Sophie L. Wang, Amil Dravid, Rulin Shao, Kevin Farhat et al. (6 authors)  ·  2610.06851

LLM ReasoningBase ModelsReinforcement Learning

TL;DR: Fixing a few starting tokens such as '.\n\nOkay' brings base-model reasoning close to that of RL-trained counterparts, which suggests RL mostly brings out behavior already learned from the training data.

Problem: It is unclear how much of the reasoning gain from RL post-training is new capability and how much is already present in base models, triggered by patterns in the training data.

Method: The authors study how pretraining data links the first tokens of a response to the reasoning style that follows. They then fix specific starting-token cues at inference and compare the base model with its RL-trained version on math and coding.

Results: Specific cues make base models competitive with their RL-trained counterparts on math and coding benchmarks, and the abstract highlights '.\n\nOkay' as one such cue.

Why it matters: This changes how to think about RL post-training and is a cheap inference-time trick worth trying before costly RL on medical vision-language models or report generators.
