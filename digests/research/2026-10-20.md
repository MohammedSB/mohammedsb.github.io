## Top Medical AI Papers (Full Summary)

### 1. [TIM: Temporal Decoupling with Iterative Mutual-Refinement Model for Longitudinal Radiology Report Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26271)
**Authors:** Yiheng Dong, Yi Lin, Shilong Huang, Xiyan Yang et al. (5 authors)  
**arXiv:** `2610.26271`  
**Tags:** Radiology Report Generation, Longitudinal Imaging, Chest X-ray

**TL;DR:** TIM decouples stable from changing findings across longitudinal exams and iteratively refines them to generate radiology reports that capture disease progression.

**Problem:** Most radiology report generation systems analyze a single timepoint and miss how disease evolves across serial examinations. Existing longitudinal approaches incorporate prior studies but do not cleanly separate persistent findings from temporal change.

**Method:** The authors propose a Temporal Decoupling with Iterative Mutual-Refinement model. Current and prior image representations are split into time-invariant and time-varying components, and the two streams refine each other over iterations before a decoder produces the report conditioned on both.

**Results:** The abstract reports improved longitudinal report generation over single-timepoint and prior longitudinal baselines, with better capture of change-related language.

**Why it matters:** This is squarely in your report generation lane and tackles the comparison-with-priors problem that dominates real chest X-ray reporting, which also stresses current evaluation metrics.


### 2. [What Language Adds, and at What Cost: Four Controlled Probes of Pathology Vision-Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26805)
**Authors:** Wenhao Zhang, Zhongliang Zhou, Yi Ping, Zirui Wang et al. (5 authors)  
**arXiv:** `2610.26805`  
**Tags:** Vision-Language Models, Histopathology, Evaluation Methodology

**TL;DR:** Four controlled probes using released checkpoints test what language actually adds to pathology vision-language models beyond their vision backbones.

**Problem:** Pathology VLMs are almost always evaluated by their own developers on self-chosen test sets, so it is unclear whether language improves performance or merely rides on the vision encoder. Published evidence is graded by how it was produced.

**Method:** The authors reuse released checkpoints and public data to run four controlled probes isolating the contribution of the language component. Each probe holds the vision backbone fixed and asks whether adding language changes downstream accuracy, robustness, or reporting quality, and at what cost.

**Results:** The abstract indicates that several published claims about language benefits weaken under controlled comparison, with language adding less than the vision backbone in some settings.

**Why it matters:** This is an evidence audit of exactly the CLIP-style medical VLM paradigm you work in, and the probe design is a template for honest ablations in radiology VLM work.


### 3. [XGLASS: X-Ray, Gaze and Language Assisted Slice Sequencing for CT Volumes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26255)
**Authors:** Michael Glöckler, Timo Ropinski  
**arXiv:** `2610.26255`  
**Tags:** Vision-Language Models, CT Imaging, Gaze-Guided Learning

**TL;DR:** XGLASS uses X-ray projections, radiologist gaze, and language cues to select and order the few CT slices a 2D VLM should read, bridging strong 2D models to 3D volumes.

**Problem:** 3D medical VLMs lag behind 2D ones because of scarce volumetric training data and 2D-centric foundation architectures. Applying 2D VLMs to CT volumes with hundreds of slices requires picking the slices that matter.

**Method:** The system sequences CT slices using three guidance signals: synthesized or paired X-ray views, eye-gaze patterns, and language prompts describing the clinical question. The chosen slice sequence is fed to a frozen 2D VLM for volumetric interpretation.

**Results:** The abstract reports that guided slice sequencing lets 2D VLMs reach competitive performance on CT tasks compared to native 3D approaches at lower data cost.

**Why it matters:** A practical route for reusing chest X-ray VLM expertise on CT, and the gaze component connects to human-in-the-loop and education-oriented radiology AI.


## Top General AI Papers (Full Summary)

### 1. [Text-to-Image Generative Modeling Without Language Pretraining](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26863)
**Authors:** Keshigeyan Chandrasegaran, Yue Zhao, Armin W. Thomas, Ehsan Adeli et al. (8 authors)  
**arXiv:** `2610.26863`  
**Tags:** Text-to-Image Generation, Multimodal Foundation Models, Autoregressive Models

**TL;DR:** Halcyon shows that early-fusion multimodal autoregressive models trained from scratch on 100M image-text pairs can beat language-pretrained text-to-image baselines.

**Problem:** Modern text-to-image systems assume internet-scale language pretraining is required for text conditioning. The paper asks whether that assumption holds.

**Method:** The authors introduce Halcyon, a family of early-fusion autoregressive models trained entirely from scratch on GPIC, a 100M image-text pair dataset. Text and image tokens are modeled jointly without any separately pretrained language encoder.

**Results:** Halcyon-0.52B outperforms LlamaGen-XL/8 on the reported generation metric, with the abstract noting consistent gains across model sizes.

**Why it matters:** Medical imaging rarely has web-scale language data, so evidence that joint image-text pretraining from scratch works at modest scale is directly relevant to building domain multimodal models.


### 2. [RoAR-VAE: Improving Latent Diffusability through Robust Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26216)
**Authors:** Yang Yue, Fangyun Wei, Yitong Wang, Jinjing Zhao et al. (7 authors)  
**arXiv:** `2610.26216`  
**Tags:** Visual Tokenizers, Latent Diffusion, Representation Learning

**TL;DR:** RoAR-VAE improves latent diffusability by keeping only the robust semantic prediction objective from REPA-E and dropping its diffusion loss and generative blocks.

**Problem:** Visual tokenizers must preserve reconstruction fidelity while producing latents that diffusion models can learn efficiently. Prior methods use semantic alignment or joint tokenizer-diffusion training, but it is unclear which ingredients matter.

**Method:** Revisiting REPA-E, the authors remove its diffusion objective and dedicated generative blocks while retaining semantic prediction, then add a robust prediction formulation. The resulting tokenizer is trained independently and plugged into standard latent diffusion.

**Results:** The abstract reports that the simplified tokenizer matches or improves diffusability and generation quality relative to the full REPA-E recipe at lower training cost.

**Why it matters:** Tokenizer design determines what latent diffusion can model, which matters for medical synthesis and self-supervised pretraining where you choose or train the encoder yourself.


### 3. [Where RL Breaks: Localizing and Repairing Failures via Targeted Continued Pretraining](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25836)
**Authors:** Xinyi Wang, Danqi Chen  
**arXiv:** `2610.25836`  
**Tags:** Reinforcement Learning, LLM Reasoning, Continued Pretraining

**TL;DR:** RLVR failures are localized to specific missing base-model knowledge, and targeted continued pretraining on those gaps repairs them.

**Problem:** Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards mostly sharpens what the base model already can do. When RL fails, it is unclear what the base model lacks.

**Method:** The authors inspect the RL policy's own rollouts and find that failure is strongly predicted by localized gaps. They then construct targeted continued-pretraining data for those gaps and retrain before RL.

**Results:** The abstract reports that targeted continued pretraining recovers performance on the failure regions where plain RL plateaued.

**Why it matters:** The diagnose-then-repair recipe applies to medical reasoning models where RL on clinical QA often stalls on knowledge the base model never saw.
