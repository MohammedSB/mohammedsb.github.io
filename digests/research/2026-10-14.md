## Top Medical AI Papers (Full Summary)

### 1. [Benchmarking Self-Supervised 3D Medical Vision Foundation Models and Transferability Estimation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17573)
**Authors:** Jiaqi Tang, Yu Wang, Yang Liu, Jing Tao et al. (10 authors)  
**arXiv:** `2610.17573`  
**Tags:** Self-Supervised Learning, 3D Medical Imaging, Foundation Models

**TL;DR:** A benchmark that compares self-supervised 3D medical vision foundation models under a single protocol and tests whether their downstream transferability can be estimated before fine-tuning.

**Problem:** There are now many self-supervised 3D medical foundation models, but published evaluations use different datasets, endpoints and fine-tuning protocols. The models also differ in architecture, pretraining objective and data, so the reported results cannot be compared directly.

**Method:** The authors evaluate many 3D medical foundation models with one shared protocol. The downstream targets range from voxel-level segmentation masks to patient-level diagnosis and outcome prediction. They also study transferability-estimation methods, which try to predict which pretrained model will work best on a given task without full fine-tuning.

**Results:** The abstract is cut off before the results. It sets out a standardized comparison and an analysis of how well transferability estimates predict which model to pick for a task.

**Why it matters:** It speaks directly to the reader's interest in self-supervised pretraining for medical imaging. It offers a fair way to compare 3D foundation models and choose one for a downstream clinical task, and could serve as a reference when evaluating new self-supervised methods.


### 2. [SPARCL: Spatially Adaptive Regional Coverage for Active Learning in Chest X-ray Analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17808)
**Authors:** Juho Jung, Seongjae Kang, Mijoo Kim, Sungduk Cho et al. (7 authors)  
**arXiv:** `2610.17808`  
**Tags:** Chest X-ray, Active Learning, Lesion Segmentation

**TL;DR:** SPARCL is an active-learning strategy for chest X-ray lesion segmentation that picks samples to annotate according to how well they cover different regions of the image.

**Problem:** Pixel-level annotation for chest X-ray lesion segmentation is expensive. Existing active-learning strategies ignore where in the image the selected samples' lesions lie, so they perform inconsistently across focal and diffuse pathologies.

**Method:** SPARCL (Spatially Adaptive Regional Coverage) adds spatial awareness to sample selection. It favours samples that improve coverage across anatomical regions, and the selection adapts to whether a pathology is spatially concentrated or spread out.

**Results:** The abstract is truncated. It reports more consistent segmentation gains across pathology types with different spatial distributions than standard active-learning baselines.

**Why it matters:** This is chest X-ray analysis aimed at reducing the cost of dense radiology labels. It is relevant to anyone building segmentation datasets for chest X-rays, including datasets for referring or grounded segmentation.


### 3. [Dissociating Answerability from Extraction in LLM Question Answering over Radiology Reports](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17576)
**Authors:** Priyanshu Sinha, Kelsey McDonald  
**arXiv:** `2610.17576`  
**Tags:** Radiology Reports, Evaluation Metrics, Clinical Question Answering

**TL;DR:** The paper proposes evaluating question answering over radiology reports by separately reporting false answers and over-abstentions, instead of folding both into one F1 score.

**Problem:** Answering a question from a radiology report involves two decisions: whether the report contains an answer, and which span gives it. RadQA results are reported as a single F1 that merges the two, which hides clinically important errors. A false answer presents an unsupported claim as fact, while an over-abstention hides what the report actually says.

**Method:** The authors propose an evaluation method that separates answerability from span extraction. They report false-answer and over-abstention rates alongside the usual extraction metrics, and use these to analyse LLM behaviour on RadQA.

**Results:** The breakdown shows that models with similar F1 can fail in quite different ways, some by fabricating answers and others by abstaining too often.

**Why it matters:** It is closely tied to the reader's work on evaluation metrics for radiology text. The idea of separating hallucinated claims from missed content also carries over to evaluating generated radiology reports.


## Top General AI Papers (Full Summary)

### 1. [Segment Where You Look: Attention as the Interface Between Language Models and Segmenters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18729)
**Authors:** Eslam Abdelrahman, Hamza Omar Alkaf, Yuheng Li, Siddharth Srinivasan Iyer et al. (8 authors)  
**arXiv:** `2610.18729`  
**Tags:** Referring Segmentation, Vision-Language Models, Attention Mechanisms

**TL;DR:** Instead of passing a single [SEG] token embedding to the mask decoder, the model uses the VLM's own attention maps from the [SEG] token to tell the decoder where the referred object is.

**Problem:** Vision-language models that segment from language usually compress the referent's location into the hidden state of one [SEG] token. That vector has no spatial index, and the mask decoder never sees the referring expression, so it has to recover the object's extent on its own.

**Method:** The authors show that the attention from the [SEG] token to image tokens already carries a spatially indexed localization signal. They use this attention as the interface to the pixel decoder, in place of or alongside the hidden-state embedding.

**Results:** The abstract reports better referring-segmentation performance than the standard [SEG]-embedding interface. Exact numbers are cut off.

**Why it matters:** This applies directly to the reader's referring-segmentation work. Using attention as the interface could be carried over to grounded segmentation in chest X-rays and other medical images built on VLM backbones.


### 2. [Rethinking Projector Training in Multimodal LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18598)
**Authors:** Fabian Gröger, Naël Ouerghemi, Shuo Wen, Maria Brbić  
**arXiv:** `2610.18598`  
**Tags:** Multimodal LLMs, Vision-Language Alignment, Training Efficiency

**TL;DR:** The paper re-examines the projector-alignment stage of multimodal LLM training and finds that a small fraction of the usual image-caption data recovers most of its benefit.

**Problem:** Multimodal LLMs are usually trained by first aligning a vision encoder to a language model through projector training on large image-caption datasets, then training the full model jointly. The role of the projector stage is poorly understood, and it can be expensive.

**Method:** The authors run controlled ablations of the projector training stage. They vary how much data it uses and study how it interacts with the later joint training stage.

**Results:** Using only 10–20% of the data for projector training already recovers most of the downstream performance. The abstract also lists further findings about what this stage contributes.

**Why it matters:** It gives practical, compute-saving guidance for building domain vision-language models, such as radiology MLLMs, where paired image-text data is scarce.


### 3. [To Align or Not To Align: Check Your COMPASS Before You Train](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18638)
**Authors:** Fabian Gröger, Petar Damjanović, Maria Brbić  
**arXiv:** `2610.18638`  
**Tags:** Model Compatibility, Vision-Language Alignment, Model Merging

**TL;DR:** COMPASS is a score that predicts, before any training, whether two pretrained models will work well when aligned or merged.

**Problem:** AI systems are often built by aligning a vision encoder with a language model or by merging fine-tuned checkpoints. Finding a compatible pair currently requires costly empirical search, because compatibility is only known after the alignment or merge is done.

**Method:** COMPASS (COMPatibility ASsessment Score) is a theoretically motivated diagnostic computed from the pretrained models' representations. It estimates how compatible two models are without running alignment or merging.

**Results:** According to the abstract, COMPASS correlates with how well models actually perform after alignment or merging, so it can screen candidate pairs cheaply.

**Why it matters:** It could help when choosing which medical or general vision encoder to pair with an LLM for CLIP-style or report-generation systems, without training every combination.
