### 1. [‘Economic D-day’: how desperate is Trump to end Iran war? — The Latest](https://www.theguardian.com/news/audio/2026/aug/25/economic-d-day-how-desperate-trump-end-iran-war-the-latest)
*The Guardian · UK · left-leaning*

**What happened:** The US Treasury, led by Secretary Scott Bessent, launched "Operation Economic Outcast" — described by Washington as an "economic D-Day" — widening sanctions on Iran's oil, petrochemical and military-linked networks and threatening secondary sanctions on any country that keeps trading with Tehran. Rubio indicated US airstrikes are halted "for the time being" as pressure shifts to the economic track, while Washington reportedly offered sanctions relief if Iran reopens the Strait of Hormuz and halts proxy attacks. China, Iran's largest oil customer, rejected the measures as illegal and vowed "all necessary measures" to protect its interests; Tehran says it is "fully prepared" to absorb them.

**Background:** The move follows a US-Israeli military campaign against Iran that has stalled short of its aims, with Iran's currency collapsing to roughly 2 million rials to the dollar. Iran has survived successive sanctions regimes since 1979 and has built deep trade and barter channels with China, Russia and regional intermediaries.

**Why it matters:** It converts a shooting war into a global economic confrontation that directly implicates China and other buyers, testing whether Washington can isolate Tehran without fracturing wider trade relations. It also signals the US is substituting financial coercion for military escalation, which will shape whether the conflict de-escalates or freezes into attrition.

**Different views:** The Jerusalem Post frames the campaign as an effective squeeze on a weakened regime and asks how long the West should let Iran rebuild its nuclear and missile programs, while noting dissident warnings that sanctions empower hardliners; the Guardian casts it as the desperate improvisation of a president whose war has failed, and Al Jazeera centers the cost to ordinary Iranians facing hyperinflation. BBC takes a middle line, questioning whether sanctions can topple a regime that has outlasted them before.


### 2. [Trump says all mines cleared from Strait of Hormuz](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwyzw836d05o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss)
*BBC Middle East · UK · mainstream*

**What happened:** Trump announced that all mines have been cleared from the Strait of Hormuz, with a US official confirming CENTCOM has reopened both inbound and outbound shipping lanes and that Space Force is monitoring the strait and Iranian nuclear sites. Separately, Iran's deputy foreign minister said Tehran and Muscat had agreed on a temporary shipping corridor through the waterway. Oil majors and Indian refiners are nonetheless avoiding vessels on Iran's blacklist and ship-to-ship transfers, keeping traffic well below normal.

**Background:** Iran effectively closed the strait — the transit route for roughly a fifth of global oil — at the start of the conflict, triggering violent swings in crude prices. Khamenei's office had called control of Hormuz "the pillar of Iran's new security order."

**Why it matters:** Reopening the strait is the single biggest variable for global energy prices and the most likely off-ramp in any US-Iran bargain. Gulf states' rush to build bypass pipelines means Iran's chokepoint leverage may be eroding permanently, changing its bargaining position for years.

**Different views:** The Jerusalem Post presents the clearance as a US operational success while stressing Iranian vows never to relinquish control, and Al Jazeera foregrounds the Iran-Oman corridor as evidence Tehran retains agency and regional partners rather than being forced open. The Guardian argues Iran faces a "Hormuz paradox" in which the closure is now hurting Tehran more than its adversaries, amplifying internal calls to end the war.


### 3. [Syria's Kurdish-led SDF dissolves as part of integration with Damascus](https://www.jpost.com/middle-east/article-906588)
*The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning*

**What happened:** SDF commander Mazloum Abdi announced the dissolution of the Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces as an independent military force, after arriving in Damascus for national unification talks, with its units to be folded into the new Syrian state's security forces. Turkey's AK Party welcomed the move and signalled a deeper alliance with Damascus, pledging help rebuilding Syrian state and military institutions.

**Background:** The SDF was the main US-backed ground force against ISIS in Syria and long governed the northeast autonomously, while Ankara treated it as an extension of the PKK. Its absorption follows the post-Assad government's push to centralise armed groups.

**Why it matters:** It removes the last major autonomous armed actor in Syria and hands Damascus — with Turkish backing — control of the northeast, redrawing the balance of power on Israel's northern periphery. It also raises questions about ISIS detention duties, Kurdish political rights, and what remains of US leverage in Syria.

**Different views:** Coverage here is largely Israeli and Turkish-facing: the Jerusalem Post treats consolidation under Damascus and Ankara as a strategic problem for Israel — especially alongside Syria's removal from the US terror list and the strike on a Turkish base — while Ankara presents it as national reunification and stabilisation.
