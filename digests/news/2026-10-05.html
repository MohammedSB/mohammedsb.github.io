1. Yemen’s Saudi-backed government launches military drive to retake all Houthi-held territory

The Guardian · UK

What happened: Rashad al-Alimi, who heads Yemen's Saudi-backed Presidential Leadership Council, announced a military operation to retake all territory still held by the Houthis. The Saudi-led coalition says it is providing air cover, and some reports put the number of fighter jets involved at 100. Government forces say they have retaken Dhubab and positions near the Bab al-Mandeb strait, while the Houthis have reportedly cut a key supply route to the besieged city of Taiz.

Background: Yemen's war has been largely frozen along fragmented front lines for years. Before this announcement, the Houthis and the Saudi-led coalition had been trading strikes.

Why it matters: This is the biggest escalation of Yemen's war in years, and it is happening next to a key global shipping chokepoint. It could also draw in Saudi Arabia's new partners in the Mecca defence pact (Pakistan and Turkey) and possibly the US.

Different views: The Guardian sets the announcement against fresh Houthi gains, including the reported encirclement of Taiz, which suggests the government is starting from a weak position. Al Jazeera leads with the government's claimed capture of Dhubab and ties it to an upcoming meeting of Saudi, Pakistani and Turkish officials, relying mainly on government statements. The Jerusalem Post consistently calls the Houthis 'Iran-backed', stresses the scale of the coalition assault, and focuses on Washington twice refusing Saudi requests for air support, though its own analysis doubts the government forces are united enough to succeed. The BBC's headline uses the president's own words, 'all-out war', and presents the move as part of an ongoing exchange of strikes.

2. Iran will not reopen Strait of Hormuz until its conditions from June are met, Ghalibaf says

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli

What happened: Iran's parliament speaker, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said Iran will not reopen the Strait of Hormuz until conditions it set in June are met. He said Washington must understand that 'the period of dragging out the process and dictating one-sided demands is over.' Separately, crude exports that bypass Hormuz rose sharply in September, but oil prices have stayed high.

Background: Iran's restrictions on traffic through Hormuz are tied to a stalled diplomatic process with the US that dates back at least to June.

Why it matters: Hormuz is the world's most important oil chokepoint, so a prolonged closure keeps global energy prices high and US–Iran tensions near breaking point. Related signals point the same way: the US abruptly withdrew its bombers from RAF Fairford over attack fears and is sending more forces to Israel.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post presents the story through Ghalibaf's hardline statement, so the issue reads as Iranian defiance and leverage over Washington. Al Jazeera treats it as a question of energy markets, asking whether Iran is quietly charging a toll on oil traffic, which would explain why prices remain high despite rising exports through other routes. Its account focuses on Gulf economic effects rather than official Iranian or US positions.

3. Flydubai co-pilot attacked captain with axe, UAE official says

BBC Middle East · UK

What happened: A UAE official says a flydubai co-pilot attacked the captain with an axe while trying to take control of an Israel-bound flight on 30 September. Passengers and crew stopped him. Several media outlets have named him as Hamam al-Hammami, an Omani national, and Australian police and security agencies are now examining the time he spent in Australia between 2015 and 2017.

Background: Flights between the UAE and Israel began after the 2020 Abraham Accords normalisation, and their security is politically sensitive given regional tensions.

Why it matters: An insider attack on an Israel-bound commercial flight raises serious questions about airline vetting and the security of Gulf–Israel air links. The investigation now spans the UAE, Oman and Australia.

Different views: The BBC relies on the UAE official's account of the attack itself. The Guardian follows the Australian angle, using RMIT University's confirmation that he left his engineering course unfinished. The Jerusalem Post foregrounds a report that flydubai hired him without a background check, even though Omani authorities had previously monitored him for visiting extremist websites, which turns the story into one of institutional negligence.
