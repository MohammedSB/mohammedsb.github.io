1. Pilot who stabbed co-pilot on Israel-bound plane may have tried to crash it, says Netanyahu

The Guardian · UK

What happened: A co-pilot on Flydubai flight FZ1073 from Dubai to Tel Aviv stabbed the captain in an apparent attempt to crash the aircraft, causing the plane to plummet 17,000 feet before passengers and off-duty pilots intervened and diverted the flight to Saudi Arabia. The attacker, an Omani national, was arrested by Saudi authorities. Israeli PM Netanyahu stated the pilot had undergone Islamist radical indoctrination and said within days it would be clear if Iran was involved.

Background: The flight carried approximately 150 mostly Israeli passengers on a route that only became possible after the Abraham Accords normalized UAE-Israel relations. Flydubai subsequently suspended all flights to Israel pending investigation.

Why it matters: The incident raises serious aviation security questions about crew vetting and screening on Israel-bound flights, and its potential terrorist dimension could strain UAE-Israel normalization ties and reignite debate about Iran's regional reach.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post foregrounds Netanyahu's framing of Islamist radicalization and possible Iran links, treating Israeli intelligence assessments as authoritative, and runs analysis noting Gulf media was slow to report details and treated Israeli sources as unreliable. The Guardian and BBC focus more on the human drama — passenger accounts, the injured pilot's heroism, and the investigative uncertainty — and are careful to note the motive remains unknown, avoiding the terrorism framing until confirmed. Al Jazeera covers the UAE investigation angle without amplifying Israeli officials' characterizations. The Guardian notes Flydubai described it as a possible mental health incident alongside hijacking as lines of inquiry, a framing absent from Jerusalem Post coverage.

2. Pentagon says it has formally completed withdrawal of US troops from Iraq

The Guardian · UK

What happened: The Pentagon formally completed the withdrawal of all US and UK troops from Iraq, ending a 12-year mission against the Islamic State that began in 2014. The withdrawal was conducted under a deal agreed two years ago following sustained pressure from the Iraqi government and Iran-aligned factions. Trump called it 'a great day for America' while Iran and its allies celebrated it as a victory.

Background: The US-led coalition entered Iraq in 2014 at Baghdad's invitation to counter the rapid territorial expansion of ISIS. Iraq's government, under pressure from Iran-backed militias and nationalist sentiment, had been demanding full sovereignty and an end to the foreign military presence for years.

Why it matters: The withdrawal marks a significant shift in the US military footprint in the Middle East and is widely seen as a geopolitical win for Iran, which has long sought to end Western military presence in neighboring Iraq. It also raises questions about Iraq's long-term security capacity against potential IS resurgence.

Different views: The Guardian leads with Trump's self-congratulatory framing but prominently notes Iran and its allies celebrated the departure as a victory, and quotes the Iraqi perspective on restored sovereignty. The Jerusalem Post runs an analysis piece framing the withdrawal with irony — noting Baghdad originally asked the US to return — and foregrounds the strategic benefit to Iran. The BBC reports it straightforwardly as the completion of a deal agreed two years ago, emphasizing it was a key Iraqi government demand. Al Jazeera's separate piece on Kurdish Peshmerga provides regional context about post-withdrawal security dynamics without directly addressing the withdrawal's geopolitical implications.

3. Iran receives US feedback on seven-day trust-building plan, main issue is sequencing

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli

What happened: Iran received US feedback via Qatari mediators on a proposed seven-day trust-building plan aimed at restarting nuclear diplomacy, with the main sticking point being the sequencing of steps each side would take. Separate talks in New York involved Qatari mediators, Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi, and a US delegation including Jared Kushner. Secretary Rubio subsequently ordered the Iranian UNGA delegation to leave the United States after negotiations stalled.

Background: The US and Iran have been engaged in indirect nuclear negotiations for months, with Qatar serving as a key intermediary. The Guardian notes the US is seven months into a war with Iran, suggesting kinetic conflict is ongoing alongside diplomacy, adding acute urgency to any diplomatic track.

Why it matters: The sequencing dispute is a classic and historically intractable obstacle in US-Iran nuclear talks, and Rubio's expulsion of the Iranian delegation signals the diplomatic window may be narrowing. A breakdown could accelerate Iran's nuclear program advancement, which Netanyahu separately told UAE President MBZ is already accelerating.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post covers this through multiple pieces emphasizing Israeli and hawkish US perspectives — a Trump aide says Iran will be 'resolved imminently' with no dilution of demands, and a former Israeli national security chief warns a US-Iran deal could still harm Israel. The Guardian's framing, referencing an ongoing US-Iran war and midterm political pressures, reflects a very different assumed baseline reality. Al Jazeera and the BBC do not appear to cover this specific diplomatic track in the provided articles, leaving it largely a Jerusalem Post and Guardian story with sharply divergent geopolitical assumptions underlying each outlet's framing.
