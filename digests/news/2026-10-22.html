1. Some US diplomats told to leave Saudi Arabia amid Houthi attacks

The Guardian · UK

What happened: The US State Department ordered some diplomats and families of government employees to leave Saudi Arabia as Houthi strikes hit the kingdom almost daily, including an attack on King Fahd Airport in Dammam and a drone strike on an oil field that together killed at least three people. Saudi Aramco told staff to evacuate its Dhahran headquarters and work remotely, and similar US withdrawals were reported from the UAE, Jordan, Qatar and Bahrain.

Background: Fighting between Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces and the Iran-allied Houthis has intensified since September, and the Houthis have widened their campaign to Saudi airports and energy infrastructure.

Why it matters: A US drawdown across several Gulf states signals Washington is bracing for a wider regional war, and sustained strikes on Saudi oil infrastructure threaten global energy supply at a moment when oil is already at a five-month high.

Different views: The Guardian and Al Jazeera tie the evacuation directly to reports of a renewed US-Israeli strike on Iran after the midterms, treating the Houthi attacks as one front in an Iran confrontation; the BBC and Jerusalem Post frame it narrowly as an escalation in the Houthi campaign, with the Post foregrounding Aramco's evacuation and oil-market impact and leaning on Saudi civil aviation and Israeli security sourcing.

2. US, Israel plan renewed Iran strikes after US midterms, focusing on short, intense attacks - report

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli

What happened: Reports say the US and Israel are planning a short, intense military operation against Iran after the US midterm elections, lasting roughly three days and including strikes on nuclear facilities. Iran's IRGC says it is operating on the assumption an attack is certain, has unveiled an 'offensive' doctrine and threatened to reveal a 'Father of All Bombs', while dozens of Israeli jets reportedly rehearsed sorties toward the Iranian border.

Background: Joint US-Israeli strikes earlier this year killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, and his son Mojtaba has not been seen publicly since late February; a separate report says Trump blocked a Netanyahu-planned multi-day operation set for 10 October.

Why it matters: A second round of strikes would reopen a direct US-Iran war with Gulf states, shipping lanes and oil markets already under strain, and the timing around US and Israeli elections makes domestic politics a driver of the decision.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post presents the strike plans in operational detail from Israeli and US security sources and treats the Iranian threats as regime bluster, while also noting Trump restrained Netanyahu; Al Jazeera foregrounds Iran's own announcements and asks whether war is imminent, framing the US evacuation orders as evidence of American intent, and separately explores Russia as a possible mediator, an option the Post does not raise.

3. How Arab parties could hold critical balance of power in Israeli election

BBC Middle East · UK

What happened: Israel votes on 27 October in its first national election since the 7 October 2023 attacks, with polls showing Netanyahu's pro-settler coalition could lose. Arab parties could hold the balance of power if turnout among Arab citizens rises, and settler attacks on Palestinian olive harvesters in the West Bank have accelerated in the run-up, including an assault that seriously injured a Washington Post photojournalist and the detention of around 80 foreign volunteers.

Background: Netanyahu has been prime minister for most of the period since 1996 and is seeking a mandate after three years of war in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran; the opposition is fractious and has struggled to present a unified alternative.

Why it matters: The outcome will shape whether Israel pursues renewed strikes on Iran, the future of settlement expansion and any path toward Saudi normalization, which Washington is pushing despite Saudi demands for Palestinian statehood.

Different views: The BBC frames the vote as a procedural and coalition-arithmetic story centred on Arab turnout; the Guardian runs opinion pieces treating a Netanyahu defeat as necessary for Israel to 'heal' and highlights far-right gains; Al Jazeera questions whether the opposition differs substantively from the right at all; the Jerusalem Post foregrounds the election's effect on Saudi normalization and reports settler violence while attributing it to a pre-election surge rather than government policy.
