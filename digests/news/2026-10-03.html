1. Flydubai co-pilot used crash axe to attack captain, says UAE

The Guardian · UK

What happened: The UAE prosecutor general said the co-pilot of flydubai flight FZ1073 from Dubai to Tel Aviv used the cockpit crash axe on the captain while trying to carry out a 'terrorist attack' and take control of the plane. Passengers and crew stepped in, and the aircraft made an emergency landing in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. Reports name the suspect as Hamam al-Hammami and say Oman had barred him from flying because of extremist views.

Background: The flight connected the UAE and Israel, which normalised relations under the Abraham Accords. The incident came during an active US-Israeli war with Iran and in the same week Netanyahu visited the UAE.

Why it matters: It is a rare attempted attack on a commercial flight on a Gulf–Israel route. It is already straining Israel-UAE aviation and security cooperation, and it tests how far the normalisation relationships can go.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post builds its account around Netanyahu: his claims that the hijacker underwent 'radical Islamist indoctrination', that the attack could have been a 'disaster on the scale of 9/11', and that those behind it 'will pay a heavy price'. It also reports Israel-UAE friction over Israel calling its flights from the Emirates 'rescue flights'. The Guardian and BBC rely mainly on the UAE prosecutor and on the Indian captain's personal story, and the Guardian also asks whether the motive was suicide or terrorism. Al Jazeera focuses on who the co-pilot was and the Omani flying ban, and none of its headlines here use Netanyahu's framing.

2. Saudis plan assault on Houthis to break Red Sea chokehold

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli

What happened: According to a Reuters report, Saudi Arabia is preparing an offensive against the Houthis in the coming weeks to break their hold on Red Sea shipping routes. Yemeni forces overseen by Riyadh would lead on the ground, backed by Saudi air strikes. The Houthis claim Saudi Arabia carried out more than 90 attacks in Yemen in 24 hours, fire and smoke were reported near an Aramco facility in Riyadh, and the Houthis have taken parts of the Red Sea coast.

Background: This is the most serious Saudi-Houthi escalation since the UN-brokered truce of 2022 largely stopped cross-border attacks. Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan, members of the 'Mecca pact', are holding emergency talks on how to deal with the Houthis politically.

Why it matters: A renewed Saudi-led ground war could reopen Yemen's war and threaten Red Sea shipping and Gulf energy facilities. The WFP says a record number of Yemenis already cannot meet their food needs.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post presents the conflict in strategic and security terms. It describes the 'Iran-aligned' Houthis attacking Saudi cities and energy sites, and Saudi Arabia planning a response to protect shipping, though it also reports Houthi claims and WFP hunger warnings. Al Jazeera tells the story from the ground: families displaced again by Houthi advances, and the Yemeni government army's claims of more than 1,500 Houthi casualties and closed crossings near Taiz. Its coverage barely mentions Iran.

3. Pentagon planning for more naval forces in Mideast as Trump weighs renewed strikes on Iran - report

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli

What happened: A report says the Pentagon is planning to send more naval forces to the Middle East while President Trump weighs renewed strikes on Iran. The US-Israeli war with Iran is now in its eighth month and US midterm elections are in November. A separate report says the US is sending Patriot missile batteries to Saudi Arabia and Qatar to protect oil and gas facilities if major combat resumes.

Background: Nuclear talks have stalled. According to diplomats quoted by Bloomberg, Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi offered inspectors access to nuclear sites in exchange for sanctions relief. The G7 is releasing 100 million barrels of oil and diesel to bring down prices pushed up by the war.

Why it matters: Renewed US strikes would end the current lull, put Gulf energy facilities at risk and could push oil prices up again just as Middle East supply has nearly returned to pre-war levels.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post depends heavily on Israeli and US officials. It quotes a senior Israeli defence official saying Iran's room to manoeuvre has narrowed, and it reads Araghchi's offer as a sign of economic weakness. Al Jazeera calls the conflict 'the US and Israel's war on Iran' and covers it mainly through its global economic cost. The BBC treats it as a supply story, explaining how US military help and pipelines that bypass the Strait of Hormuz have brought oil exports back.
