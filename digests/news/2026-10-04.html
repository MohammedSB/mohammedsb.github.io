1. Flydubai co-pilot used crash axe to attack captain, says UAE

The Guardian · UK

What happened: The UAE prosecutor general says the co-pilot of Israel-bound flydubai flight FZ1073 attacked the captain with a crash axe and tried to take control of the aircraft. The UAE is calling it an attempted terrorist attack. Passengers and crew stepped in, but only after the jet went into a near-vertical dive of about 17,000 feet. The suspect, named in media reports as Omani pilot Hamam al-Hammami, had reportedly been barred from flying by Oman over extremist views, and Australian police and security agencies are now looking into the roughly two years he spent there as a student.

Background: The flight was going from the UAE to Israel, a route that exists because of the normalisation of ties between the two countries. Reports say flydubai hired the pilot even though Oman had concerns about his radicalisation.

Why it matters: It is the most serious security incident yet on a Gulf–Israel air route. It raises questions about how airlines vet pilots and about how security and intelligence are shared between the UAE, Oman, Israel and Australia.

Different views: The Guardian and BBC build their reports mainly on the UAE prosecutor's statements and the Australian investigation into the co-pilot's past, and treat the UAE account as the main source. Al Jazeera runs a piece on the questions being raised about the official versions and doesn't accept the official narrative as settled. The Jerusalem Post centres the Israeli angle: Netanyahu's call with the captain, a source saying Israel had the full crew and passenger list before takeoff, and the WSJ-sourced claim that flydubai hired the pilot without a background check.

2. Yemen’s leader announces military operation to retake Houthi-held territory

Al Jazeera English · Qatar

What happened: Rashad al-Alimi, head of Yemen's Presidential Leadership Council, has announced a military operation to retake the remaining territory held by the Houthis. Government-aligned forces have since reported strikes on Houthi strongholds, including the capital, Sanaa. Saudi Arabia and the Houthis are also trading claims of strikes on each other's territory.

Background: Yemen's war has been largely frozen for several years, with the Houthis holding Sanaa and much of the populous north. The internationally recognised government is backed by a Saudi-led coalition.

Why it matters: A full government ground offensive would restart one of the region's deadliest wars and could draw Saudi Arabia further in. A Jerusalem Post report says it could also draw in the US, which Riyadh has reportedly asked for air support. The fighting would also add to instability around the Red Sea.

Different views: Al Jazeera presents the campaign in the government's own terms, as a bid to 'restore state authority'. It covers civilians trapped in Houthi-held Mocha and reports the coalition's rejection of Houthi strike claims. The Jerusalem Post consistently calls the Houthis 'Iran-backed' and covers the offensive as part of a wider regional struggle, with reports on possible US involvement, escalating Saudi-Houthi attacks and links to conflicts in the Horn of Africa.

3. Iran says Hormuz to remain closed until US meets conditions

Al Jazeera English · Qatar

What happened: Iran says the Strait of Hormuz will stay closed until the US meets conditions Tehran laid out in June. Parliament speaker Ghalibaf said Washington must accept that 'dragging out' diplomacy and 'dictating one-sided demands' is over. US Defense Secretary Hegseth responded that Washington is 'hell-bent' on stopping Iran from getting nuclear weapons, that all military options remain open, and that the US controls the strait.

Background: The closure follows the US–Iran confrontation of recent months. Talks have stalled over different priorities: Tehran wants the strait issue settled, while Washington is focused on Iran's nuclear programme.

Why it matters: A large share of the world's seaborne oil and LNG passes through Hormuz. A long closure, and the two sides' conflicting claims to control it, keep the risk of renewed military escalation and global energy disruption high.

Different views: Al Jazeera frames the standoff as a mismatch of priorities and gives Tehran's position, that reopening the strait is its main goal, equal weight to the US focus on nuclear talks. The Jerusalem Post quotes Ghalibaf's hardline wording but surrounds it with US-sourced coverage: Hegseth's claim of US control and his warning that Iran is rebuilding, plus a US-led initiative to block weapons reaching Iran.
