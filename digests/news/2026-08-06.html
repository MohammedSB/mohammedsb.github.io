1. Understanding reached on coordinates for shipping route through Hormuz, Iran says

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Iran's foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said Tehran and Muscat have reached an understanding on the geographic coordinates of a shipping route through the Strait of Hormuz, with a joint Iran-Oman announcement being finalised so long as "certain third parties" do not interfere. Reporting indicates the arrangement would have Iran controlling or coordinating inbound traffic, and Tehran cautioned that the deal alone would not guarantee the waterway's security. Trump publicly dismissed the diplomacy, saying Iran had lied about negotiations and that the strait would open "very soon, or they're going to get hit very hard," while traffic remains at roughly eight transits versus a normal ~130 per day.

Background: The US-Israeli war on Iran has been accompanied by an effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint for roughly a fifth of global oil, with Oman acting as the traditional back-channel mediator between Washington and Tehran.

Why it matters: A Hormuz arrangement is the single lever that would unblock global energy flows and could either become the scaffolding for a wider ceasefire or the trigger for renewed US strikes if Washington rejects any Iranian role in policing the waterway.

Different views: Al Jazeera and the Guardian foreground the deal as genuinely "close" and treat Trump's threats as erratic obstruction (one Guardian columnist calls it "kindergarten diplomacy"), while the Jerusalem Post emphasises the concession embedded in the text — that ships must coordinate with Iran even on the Omani side — and leads with Trump's claim that Iran lied about talks; US officials in all versions insist no arrangement leaving Iran in control is acceptable.

2. Israel strikes south Lebanon after two soldiers killed by explosion

BBC Middle East · UK · mainstream

What happened: Israel carried out airstrikes across southern Lebanon after two Israeli soldiers were killed by an explosion, its first military fatalities in Lebanon in over a month. The strikes hit two towns including a cemetery in Tebnine, killing at least one person and wounding 11, among the deadliest Israeli bombardments since the June ceasefire took effect. The IDF said it issued evacuation warnings beforehand — the first such warnings since June — and described the operation as precise retaliation for Hezbollah ceasefire violations.

Background: A ceasefire has nominally held in Lebanon since June but has been punctuated by near-daily Israeli strikes and disputes over Hezbollah's rearmament, amid Lebanese government pressure on the group to disarm.

Why it matters: With the US-Iran war still running, an escalation on the Lebanese front risks reopening a second major theatre and collapsing the one ceasefire in the region that has partially held.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post frames the strikes as "precise" retaliation for Hezbollah violations and stresses the advance evacuation warnings; the Guardian leads on the civilian toll and the cemetery hit as among the deadliest bombings of the ceasefire; the BBC gives roughly equal weight to the soldiers' deaths and the Lebanese casualty; separately, rights groups' war-crime finding on an earlier strike that killed a Lebanese journalist is amplified by the Guardian and largely absent from Israeli coverage.

3. Trump’s Gaza Board of Peace issues first building contract – for a military base

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: According to a Guardian exclusive, Trump's Gaza "Board of Peace" — created in January to oversee reconstruction — has issued its first construction contract, awarded to a Louisiana firm. The project is not housing or civilian infrastructure but a rudimentary 150-person military outpost to accommodate Moroccan troops.

Background: The Board of Peace was set up under the Trump-brokered Gaza framework to channel reconstruction and stand up an international stabilisation force; Morocco was the first African state to join it, and a roadmap to the ceasefire's second phase has just been announced.

Why it matters: The first concrete act of Gaza "reconstruction" being a foreign military base signals that securitisation, not rebuilding, is the near-term priority, and it commits Arab troops to policing a territory where Hamas is already threatening collaborators.

Different views: The Guardian presents the contract as evidence that reconstruction has been hollowed out in favour of a security mission, while Israeli coverage of phase two focuses instead on Hamas pressuring Gazans to assassinate members of Israeli-backed militias, casting an international force as necessary rather than as displaced reconstruction.
