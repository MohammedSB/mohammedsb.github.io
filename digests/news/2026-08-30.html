1. Six months on, what did the strikes on Iran achieve?

Al Jazeera English · Qatar · pro-Palestinian

What happened: A detailed accounting of the six-month-old US-Israeli military strikes on Iran finds the campaign reshaped the strategic landscape around the Strait of Hormuz, with Iran keeping the waterway restricted and diplomacy stalled. AJLabs has tallied the human, economic, and geopolitical costs of the conflict. The war continues to reverberate across all three governments involved.

Background: The US and Israel launched strikes on Iran in what was framed as a campaign to neutralize Iran's nuclear and military capabilities. The Strait of Hormuz, through which a large share of global oil passes, became a central flashpoint as Iran responded by restricting transit.

Why it matters: A restricted Strait of Hormuz has global energy and economic consequences, and the stalled diplomacy signals a prolonged standoff with no clear off-ramp. The six-month mark is a key moment to assess whether the strikes achieved their stated objectives.

Different views: Al Jazeera frames the strikes as a costly US-Israeli 'bombing of Iran' with heavy humanitarian and geopolitical consequences, emphasizing the suffering of ordinary Iranians (see article 9) and the failure to achieve strategic goals. The Jerusalem Post's coverage (articles 35, 34) focuses on sanctions pressure weakening the Iranian regime and Khamenei's diminished public presence, framing the campaign in terms of whether it can produce regime change — a more optimistic read for the coalition.

2. Turkish, Saudi, Pakistani officials to meet for first Mecca defense pact summit, source says

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: Turkish, Saudi, and Pakistani officials are set to convene for the first summit under a nascent 'Mecca defense pact,' according to a Turkish foreign ministry source, with the meeting scheduled for Monday. The three countries are reportedly alarmed by the potential for further escalation of the Iran war. The gathering signals a realignment of Sunni-majority powers in response to the regional conflict.

Background: The US-Israeli strikes on Iran have unsettled the broader Middle East, prompting regional powers to recalibrate their security postures. Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan all have complex relationships with both the US-Israeli coalition and Iran.

Why it matters: A formal defense coordination mechanism among Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan would represent a significant new security architecture in the Islamic world, with implications for how the Iran conflict could expand or be contained. It also reflects the limits of US influence over regional allies' independent security calculations.

3. Iran’s Khamenei calls for Gulf unity as Hormuz tensions persist

Al Jazeera English · Qatar · pro-Palestinian

What happened: Iran's Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, who has not been seen publicly in months, issued a message to Gulf State rulers calling for unity among Muslims and urging them to identify their 'real enemy' as Hormuz tensions persist and diplomacy remains deadlocked. The message was issued to coincide with the Prophet Muhammad's birth anniversary. The Jerusalem Post notes Khamenei's prolonged absence from public view.

Background: Khamenei's public invisibility amid an active war has fueled speculation about his health and political grip. The Strait of Hormuz remains partially restricted by Iran, placing enormous pressure on Gulf economies and global oil markets.

Why it matters: Khamenei's appeal to Gulf rulers to close ranks against a common enemy — implicitly the US and Israel — tests whether the war is driving a wedge between Gulf states and Washington or pushing them closer together. His message, issued remotely, also raises questions about Iranian leadership continuity.

Different views: Al Jazeera presents Khamenei's message as a substantive diplomatic overture aimed at building Islamic solidarity against Western-Israeli aggression. The Jerusalem Post highlights his months-long disappearance from public view, framing the message skeptically as a sign of weakness or regime instability rather than a credible diplomatic initiative.
