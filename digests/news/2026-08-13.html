1. 'I think we'll keep it': Trump says US controls Hormuz, IDF believes blockade is 'highly effective'

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: President Trump declared on Truth Social that the US has "total control" of the Strait of Hormuz and said "I think we'll keep it," while IDF Chief of Staff Lt.-Gen. Eyal Zamir told Israeli ministers the blockade of Iran is "highly effective" and that Iran's economic crisis is deepening. Tehran publicly rejected the claim, asserting its own control of the waterway, and a senior Iranian official said no talks are under way on extending the 60-day ceasefire framework that was meant to produce a nuclear deal and sanctions relief.

Background: The US-Iran conflict has left the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint for roughly a fifth of global oil flows — militarized, with US embassies across the region on reduced operations since diplomats and families were ordered out months ago. Iran has also just reshuffled its top military and IRGC command.

Why it matters: A naval blockade at Hormuz with no diplomatic track running risks either an economic strangulation of Iran or a miscalculation that reopens full-scale war, with immediate consequences for global energy markets and Gulf states. The stalled 60-day clock means the nuclear file is drifting toward expiry without a deal.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post foregrounds Trump's control claim and IDF assessments of Iranian economic collapse, treating the blockade as a working pressure strategy, and separately reports Iranian missile tactics depleting US Patriot stocks; Al Jazeera frames the same standoff as a "deadlock" in which mediators are struggling to coax both sides back to talks, centering diplomatic failure rather than Iranian weakness, while Iranian officials deny US dominance outright.

2. Israeli militants besiege two Palestinian families in their West Bank homes

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Israeli settlers, in some accounts accompanied by soldiers, have besieged two to three Palestinian families in their homes in the West Bank village of Qusra since Sunday, pitching a tent outside one house and cutting off water and electricity while blocking food, supplies and medical access. Israeli security forces reportedly withdrew, leaving about a dozen settlers at the site, and additional troops were subsequently ordered to the village. A US diplomat described the siege as an "act of terror."

Background: Qusra has been a repeated flashpoint for settler violence, and settler efforts to seize Palestinian homes and land in the occupied West Bank have accelerated with limited enforcement by Israeli authorities.

Why it matters: The episode tests whether Israeli forces will act against settlers taking over Palestinian property, and open US criticism signals friction between Washington and Israel over West Bank annexation-by-attrition.

Different views: The Guardian calls the perpetrators "Israeli militants" and describes a deliberate "campaign of terror" involving both settlers and soldiers; Al Jazeera emphasizes the siege as collective punishment and leads on the US diplomat's "act of terror" language; the BBC uses more cautious attribution ("say," "reportedly") and frames the story around the deployment of additional troops as a security response rather than state complicity.

3. Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan to build joint political and military pact mechanisms

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan are building out the mechanisms of the "Mecca Joint Defense Agreement" signed on August 7, including a grouping of ministers, joint military exercises and deeper defense-industry cooperation. Ankara laid out the implementation framework publicly, positioning the three Sunni-majority US partners as a bloc.

Background: The pact follows Iranian missile fire reaching Gulf territory during the current conflict, which exposed the limits of existing US security guarantees for Riyadh. Saudi Arabia has spent three years trying to contain the Houthis without returning to full-scale war.

Why it matters: A Turkey-Saudi-Pakistan defense architecture — with Pakistan's nuclear status hovering in the background — would be the most significant regional realignment in years and reduces Gulf dependence on Washington alone for deterrence.

Different views: Al Jazeera presents the pact largely in institutional terms — ministers, exercises, industrial cooperation — as legitimate collective self-defense; the Jerusalem Post frames it explicitly as a Sunni-Muslim bloc formed in reaction to Iranian missile fire, and pairs it with analysis of Riyadh's dilemma of deterring Tehran without being dragged into war.
