1. Israeli police arrest 10 over settler attacks on journalist and diplomats in West Bank

BBC Middle East · UK

What happened: Israeli police arrested 10 people after masked settlers attacked a delegation of European diplomats, French clerics and a visiting French mayor, and separately beat Washington Post photojournalist Heidi Levine, during the West Bank olive harvest. US Ambassador Mike Huckabee called the Levine attack 'terror' and welcomed the arrests, while US diplomats visited the Palestinian Christian town where the violence occurred.

Background: Settler violence against Palestinians and foreign visitors spikes every olive harvest season, and the attacks come as Israel heads toward an election in which far-right parties tied to the settler movement are expected to gain.

Why it matters: Attacks on Western diplomats and an American journalist have turned settler violence from a Palestinian grievance into a direct diplomatic problem for Netanyahu with Washington and European capitals, and the arrests test whether Israel will prosecute settlers when the victims are foreigners.

Different views: The Guardian and Al Jazeera foreground evidence contradicting the official Israeli account, with the Guardian publishing its own reporter's footage of masked attackers and Al Jazeera analysing coordinates and timelines, both treating Netanyahu's 'serious accusations require serious evidence' response as the thing to be rebutted. The BBC gives equal weight to the military's condemnation and the prime minister's office's 'smear' claim, while the Jerusalem Post foregrounds the US diplomatic visit and police response rather than the question of whether Israeli accounts are credible.

2. Iran support for Houthis probed as Yemen war re-erupts and Saudi cities hit

Al Jazeera English · Qatar

What happened: Houthi ballistic missiles struck a Yemeni government training camp in Shabwa governorate, killing at least 18 and possibly dozens, as the Houthis also carried out a wave of attacks on Saudi cities. Egypt's parliament is weighing a vote to authorise deploying troops to defend Saudi Arabia, and new reports point to renewed Iranian support for the Houthis.

Background: Yemen's war had been largely frozen under a Saudi-Houthi truce, but it has re-erupted as part of the wider regional war involving Iran, the US and Israel, with the Strait of Hormuz disrupted and oil prices soaring.

Why it matters: A Gulf state under direct missile attack with Egypt considering intervention would widen the Iran war into a new front, and Saudi Arabia's difficulty securing US protection is reshaping its calculations about whom to rely on.

Different views: Al Jazeera frames the escalation through Iranian backing and the Saudi-Houthi dynamic, drawing on Yemeni government spokesmen. The Guardian runs a column arguing the key story is Trump refusing Mohammed bin Salman's calls for help despite years of transactional deal-making. The Jerusalem Post foregrounds the opening for Israel to help Saudi Arabia defend itself and treats Egyptian deployment as a potential escalation of the 'wider Iran war', a framing the other outlets do not adopt.

3. Funding shortages force World Food Programme to cut aid to Gaza

BBC Middle East · UK

What happened: The UN World Food Programme is cutting monthly food assistance in Gaza from 1.6 million to 1.1 million people because of funding shortages, ending support for roughly 500,000 Palestinians. The agency's Palestine director warned the cuts will deepen hunger this winter.

Background: More than a year after the Trump-brokered ceasefire, two-thirds of Gaza's population still faces crisis levels of food insecurity, and donor funding for UN agencies has fallen sharply across the board.

Why it matters: The cuts threaten to reverse the fragile food-security gains made since the ceasefire and risk pushing parts of Gaza back toward famine conditions, shifting the humanitarian crisis from one driven by blockade to one driven by donor fatigue.

Different views: The Guardian leads with famine risk, citing 200,000 people in danger and framing the cuts as a product of 'sweeping aid cuts' by donors. The BBC and Jerusalem Post report the numbers neutrally and attribute the decision to WFP's own funding shortfall without assigning responsibility, while Al Jazeera emphasises the agency's warning that 'modest and fragile' progress is at risk.
