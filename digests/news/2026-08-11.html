1. Iran, US set new conditions during Hormuz talks: What does this mean?

Al Jazeera English · Qatar · pro-Palestinian

What happened: Talks between Washington and Tehran over ending the war and reopening the Strait of Hormuz have stalled, with both sides attaching new conditions and oil prices surging as a result. Trump publicly demanded that Iran pay compensation for decades of US soldier deaths and for Iranians killed by the IRGC, while claiming the US Navy alone controls the strait and has mine-swept it in full. Iran, meanwhile, is pressing demands it says were already conceded in an earlier memorandum, and the US struck a Panamanian-flagged vessel in the Gulf of Oman for allegedly breaching the blockade on Iran.

Background: The closure of Hormuz followed Israeli-US strikes that killed much of Iran's senior military leadership, after which Khamenei named a new IRGC chief and filled six top posts. Roughly a fifth of global oil transits the strait, and Gulf states import much of their food through it.

Why it matters: A prolonged closure is already inflicting costs far beyond the belligerents — Gulf food and energy prices, Iraqi Kurdistan's trade, and global shipping insurance — and each new precondition pushes a negotiated reopening further away while raising the odds of further naval incidents.

Different views: Al Jazeera and the Guardian frame the impasse as a consequence of Trump's escalating and shifting demands reaching an 'impasse,' while the Jerusalem Post frames it as Iran cynically withholding a deal it already agreed to in order to deny Trump a victory, and highlights the economic 'choke' on Tehran as leverage that is working.

2. Netanyahu's rejection of Trump's 15-point Gaza plan unlikely to be final word

BBC Middle East · UK · mainstream

What happened: Benjamin Netanyahu publicly rejected Trump's 15-point Gaza plan, which paired phased Hamas disarmament with Israeli military withdrawal. Reporting indicates the White House is unbothered, treating the rejection as pre-election campaign rhetoric, and that Netanyahu had personally been working through details of a pilot withdrawal only days earlier. Israeli attacks in Gaza slowed sharply over the same week.

Background: Trump announced in late July that a 'monumental' US-backed Gaza deal had been reached, but no implementation followed. Netanyahu is caught between far-right coalition partners who reject any withdrawal and a US administration seeking a deliverable ahead of an Israeli general election.

Why it matters: Whether the plan survives determines both the prospect of sustained humanitarian relief in Gaza and whether Washington is willing to convert rhetorical pressure on Israel into actual leverage.

Different views: The BBC casts the rejection as tactical and probably not final; the Guardian's editorial line treats it as proof Netanyahu never intended a deal and that Trump's claims are hollow without real US pressure, with a news analysis emphasizing his 'flip-flopping' between incompatible allies; Al Jazeera frames it as Netanyahu 'hedging' while the underlying siege and killing continue.

3. Ousted Syrian dictator Bashar al-Assad sentenced to death in absentia

BBC Middle East · UK · mainstream

What happened: A Syrian court sentenced ousted president Bashar al-Assad to death in absentia for war crimes and crimes against humanity, including premeditated murder of children, torture, and arbitrary detention. His brother Maher was sentenced alongside him, as was former official Atef Najib. Both brothers fled to Moscow after being toppled and are beyond the court's reach.

Background: The verdict covers atrocities from Syria's 14-year civil war, in which roughly 500,000 people died. It is the most prominent case yet brought by the post-Assad transitional authorities under Ahmed al-Sharaa.

Why it matters: This is the central test of whether Syria's transitional justice process is credible and rule-bound or largely symbolic, and it shapes both domestic legitimacy and the willingness of outside states to normalize with Damascus.

Different views: Al Jazeera and the Guardian foreground the verdict as a milestone for transitional justice and victims' accountability; the Jerusalem Post reports the convictions in detail but sits alongside commentary casting al-Sharaa as a former jihadist whose intentions should not be trusted, and pairs the story with Syrian Kurdish unrest and the nuclear handover as tests of the new government.
