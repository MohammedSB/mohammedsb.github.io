1. Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey sign Nato-style defence pact amid deadly Houthi strikes in Yemen

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey signed a mutual defence agreement in Mecca stipulating that an attack on one will be treated as an attack on all, a NATO-style commitment binding three of the Muslim world's most powerful states. The pact was concluded as Riyadh faces escalating attacks from the Iran-aligned Houthis in Yemen, who also struck Marib again this week. Pakistan's nuclear arsenal makes the guarantee unusually weighty for Gulf security.

Background: Saudi Arabia has long relied on the US security umbrella, but confidence in Washington has eroded through the Iran war and renewed Houthi strikes, while Turkey and Pakistan have both sought expanded regional roles.

Why it matters: It marks the most concrete step yet toward a Sunni-led regional security architecture built outside US guarantees, and implicitly extends a nuclear-armed state's deterrence toward the Gulf.

Different views: Al Jazeera frames it as evidence of a 'post-American Middle East' and a legitimate new security order; BBC reports it flatly as a defence pact amid regional conflict; the Jerusalem Post counters with an Israeli ex-national security official dismissing Saudi Arabia as a militarily hollow 'paper tiger', downplaying the pact's real capability.

2. Iran plots to deal Trump a blow in midterms by keeping him entangled in war

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Tehran has reportedly adopted a deliberately protracted negotiating timetable intended to keep Donald Trump entangled in the Iran conflict through the US midterm elections, hoping to inflict Carter-style political damage. This comes as the US maintains a blockade that has reportedly halted Iranian oil exports from Kharg Island since 31 July, and as Washington says a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz is close. The IRGC insists Hormuz reopening depends on talks with the US, not on Oman's mediation.

Background: The US-Israel war on Iran is now in its sixth month, with Trump cycling through threats and partial withdrawals without securing a decisive military outcome.

Why it matters: The conflict's trajectory is now tied to the US electoral calendar, and Hormuz closure keeps global energy markets hostage to a negotiation neither side can afford to lose.

Different views: The Guardian portrays Trump as strategically ignorant and trapped in an 'impotent cycle' of empty threats, with Iran holding the initiative; the Jerusalem Post emphasises Iranian weakness — collapsed oil exports, an economy squeezed by blockade — and presents the US as negotiating from strength; Al Jazeera's regional framing casts the whole episode as accelerating American decline in the region.

3. Israel, Lebanon agree on countries to monitor Hezbollah disarmament deal

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: Israel and Lebanon have agreed on which countries will monitor a deal to disarm Hezbollah, a significant procedural breakthrough in the US-brokered effort to end fighting that has raged since March. The agreement follows talks in Rome, some sessions of which ended early after Israeli strikes on Hezbollah infrastructure in Lebanon.

Background: Washington has been pressing for a framework ending the Israel-Hezbollah war and disarming the Iran-backed group, a demand that cuts to the core of Lebanon's internal power balance.

Why it matters: Agreed monitoring is the mechanism on which any disarmament deal stands or falls, and would represent the first serious attempt to dismantle Hezbollah's arsenal through negotiation rather than force.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post frames the talks as succeeding despite Hezbollah's sabotage attempts and amplifies Lebanese Christian voices (Kataeb's Sami Gemayel) calling Shiites 'victims of Hezbollah' — a framing that presents disarmament as internally desired; pro-Palestinian coverage tends to read the same process through the lens of Israeli strikes continuing during negotiations and external pressure on Lebanese sovereignty.
