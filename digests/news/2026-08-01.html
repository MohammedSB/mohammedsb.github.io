1. Trump says ‘complete disarmament’ of Hamas agreed in Gaza but Israel yet to accept

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Donald Trump announced what he called a breakthrough in which Hamas agreed to "complete disarmament" in Gaza in exchange for an Israeli military withdrawal, and the Trump-chaired "Board of Peace" published a full roadmap for implementing the deal. Israel has not formally accepted the framework: Netanyahu's government voiced deep scepticism, far-right ministers said the "only solution" remains destroying Hamas and encouraging emigration, and Trump declined to say whether withdrawal or disarmament comes first. Hamas negotiators say the package is a broader framework than disarmament alone and warn they will walk away if Israel does not sign on.

Background: Implementation of Trump's Gaza peace plan has been stalled for months over the twin questions of Hamas's weapons and an Israeli pullback, after nearly three years of war that devastated the territory. Disarmament has been Hamas's declared red line throughout previous rounds of mediation.

Why it matters: If it holds, it would be the first credible step toward ending the Gaza war by removing the single biggest obstacle to the peace process; if sequencing collapses, it becomes another failed announcement that hardens both sides.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post and US officials frame it as a "historic breakthrough" in which Hamas capitulated and Israel need only wait — while stressing there will be no withdrawal "as long as there is a Hamas threat." Al Jazeera and the Guardian instead foreground Palestinian scepticism on the ground and Netanyahu's coalition turmoil, casting Israeli withdrawal rather than Hamas's arms as the real open question, while the BBC's Bowen takes a middle line: "rare hope" but big obstacles and zero trust.

2. Iran strikes tankers under US escort in Hormuz strait as Trump gathers cabinet to discuss war

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Iran's Revolutionary Guard said it struck two tankers transiting the Strait of Hormuz under US naval escort, with UKMTO reporting projectile and explosion incidents off Oman and Kuwait saying it intercepted Iranian drones. Trump convened his cabinet at Camp David over the stalling war and surging energy prices, as reports emerged that Washington and Israel are weighing a wave of strikes on Iranian energy infrastructure and even a land blockade of Iran. The conflict has now spread into at least five other countries, with Saudi Arabia joining airstrikes in Iraq — which said it had no prior knowledge of the attacks.

Background: Fighting between the US and Iran resumed roughly two weeks ago after an earlier round, and Tehran has responded by targeting the shipping chokepoint that carries a large share of the world's seaborne oil. Iranian media say a retaliation plan is ready if US strikes hit civilian energy sites.

Why it matters: Attacks on escorted tankers and the threat to energy infrastructure risk an oil-price and recession shock that is already reshaping US midterm politics, while dragging Gulf states, Iraq and Egypt into a war they tried to avoid.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post covers it primarily as an Israeli-American strategic deliberation — target sets, blockade options, Iranian drone threats to Kuwait, and a Trump-Netanyahu divide over the fortified Pickaxe Mountain site. Al Jazeera leads with Iran's warning against fanning the "flames of war" and frames the prospective targets as civilian energy sites, while the Guardian's commentary presents the whole campaign as a US war spinning out of control after failing to topple the Iranian government.

3. At least 57 people die as tens of thousands cross to Spanish enclave of Ceuta from Morocco

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: At least 57 people died — Al Jazeera and Spanish officials later cited 67 — as up to 50,000 migrants crossed from Morocco into the small Spanish enclave of Ceuta over a matter of days. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called it a "violation of Spain's territorial integrity," and after other EU states reacted by tightening their own borders he demanded an urgent meeting of EU interior ministers, denouncing a "selfish, polarising and unlawful reaction." Madrid says the migrants have since returned to Morocco and insists Schengen's integrity is guaranteed.

Background: Ceuta is one of two Spanish enclaves on the North African coast and the EU's only land border with Morocco, which has repeatedly been accused of easing or tightening migration pressure as diplomatic leverage. Previous mass crossings have coincided with Madrid-Rabat disputes over Western Sahara.

Why it matters: It is the deadliest single Ceuta crossing on record and has triggered an intra-EU border crisis, putting Spain's relationship with Morocco and the credibility of Schengen under simultaneous strain.

Different views: Coverage is only mildly contested: the Guardian emphasises the death toll and EU capitals' criticism of Madrid, while Al Jazeera's follow-ups foreground the Spanish government's reassurances that the migrants returned to Morocco and that Schengen is intact — with Rabat's own role in permitting the surge largely unexamined in both.
