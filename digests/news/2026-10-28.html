1. Coalition talks loom in Israel as Netanyahu and Eisenkot blocs set to fall short of majority

The Guardian · UK

What happened: Israel's general election produced no clear winner, with both Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc and Gadi Eisenkot's opposition bloc projected to fall short of the 61 seats needed to govern. Turnout was the highest in decades, and parties representing Palestinian citizens of Israel made large gains, leaving them positioned as potential kingmakers. Coalition talks now loom, with a repeat election possible if neither side will rely on Arab parties.

Background: This is the first election since the 7 October 2023 attacks and the Gaza war, and was widely described as the most consequential in Israel's history. Netanyahu's far-right partners, including Itamar Ben-Gvir, appear to have been among the night's losers.

Why it matters: Whether a government can be formed with Arab party support, or Israel returns to the polls, will shape Gaza's future, relations with Iran and the Gulf, and the viability of Netanyahu's political survival. Markets reacted immediately, with the Tel Aviv 125 index falling more than 3 percent.

Different views: Al Jazeera foregrounds the historic turnout of Palestinian citizens of Israel as a direct threat to Netanyahu and frames the stalemate as a test of whether Israeli parties will break the 'taboo' on Arab allies; the BBC frames the same arithmetic more neutrally as both blocs needing 'reliance on Arab parties'; the Guardian splits between a news line that Palestinian parties 'surged' and an opinion column by Dahlia Scheindlin arguing Netanyahu 'survives for now' and the centre-left's prospects are bleak once the real count replaced the exit polls. Notably, none of the Jerusalem Post items in this set cover the election at all, so the Israeli right-leaning outlet's framing is absent here.

2. Gulf states working on multipart peace deal to avert renewal of US-Iran war

The Guardian · UK

What happened: Gulf states are working on a multipart peace deal to avert a renewed US-Iran war, centred on reopening the Strait of Hormuz within four days of sanctions being lifted and on the Houthis withdrawing substantial forces inside Yemen. Qatari mediators have presented a proposal to Tehran, while Qatar publicly says Iran must reopen the strait and halt threats against Gulf states for mediation to succeed. Iran's security chief briefed Khamenei after talks with the Mecca pact countries, with Khamenei stressing Iran's conditions for ending the war.

Background: Iran has been attacking and restricting shipping in Hormuz since US and Israeli airstrikes on 28 February, and an oil spill from a damaged tanker has now closed UAE beaches. The Houthis claim to have struck Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery and defence ministry with ballistic missiles, widening the regional front.

Why it matters: A Gulf-brokered package linking Hormuz, sanctions relief and Yemen would be the most ambitious attempt yet to end a war that is choking global energy flows and threatening Gulf infrastructure. Failure risks a resumption of US and Israeli strikes, which officials say could come this week.

Different views: The Guardian foregrounds the Gulf states' own plan and treats their fear of a 'disastrous' renewed war as the organising frame; the Jerusalem Post foregrounds pressure on Iran, pairing Qatar's demand that Tehran 'show good intentions' with US Treasury Secretary Bessent's forecast of a further 25 percent economic contraction and Khamenei's insistence on Iran's conditions, and it also carries the Houthi strike claims on Saudi Arabia; Al Jazeera's contribution here is the environmental fallout, the Hormuz oil spill closing UAE beaches, rather than the diplomacy itself.

3. Jordan carries out deadly strike in Syria in retaliation for church attack

BBC Middle East · UK

What happened: Jordan carried out air strikes in southern Syria's Daraa province in retaliation for a deadly attack on a church in Jordan, killing civilians according to Syrian health officials. Damascus condemned the strikes as a violation of its sovereignty, denied that they were coordinated with Jordan, and opened an investigation, while Jordan says it hit ISIS-linked hideouts posing an imminent threat. The two governments have since moved to set up a joint security committee to reduce tensions.

Background: The strikes follow Friday's attack on a Greek Orthodox church in Jordan, which Patriarch Theophilos III attributes to a 'minority group of radicals'. Post-Assad Syria under Ahmed al-Sharaa is simultaneously courting Iraq and other neighbours for trade and security ties.

Why it matters: This is a rare cross-border military action by Jordan against its northern neighbour and a test of whether the new Syrian government can police jihadist activity in Daraa without provoking further unilateral strikes. The dispute over coordination exposes how fragile Amman-Damascus trust remains.

Different views: Al Jazeera leads with the victims, reporting at least seven dead including three children and profiling them individually; the BBC gives a lower Syrian official toll of five and frames the strike primarily as retaliation for the church attack; the Jerusalem Post foregrounds Jordan's claim of more than 20 'terrorists' killed and the de-escalation committee, and runs a separate piece on 'Arab conspiracy theorists' blaming Israel for both the strikes and the church attack, treating Jordan's account as the credible one while noting Damascus's denial of coordination.
