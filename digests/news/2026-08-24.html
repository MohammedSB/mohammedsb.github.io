1. Iran faces 'greatest financial offensive ever', says US treasury secretary

BBC Middle East · UK · mainstream

What happened: US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced what he called "the single greatest financial offensive ever marshalled against an adversary," pledging to sever all remaining economic ties to Iran and warning that any country or entity maintaining financial links to Tehran will itself face isolation. Trump separately claimed Iran is "completely collapsing," while Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi dismissed the measures as "desperate" and Tehran vowed to retaliate against any state that cooperates. Reporting points to broadened secondary sanctions, possible targets including Chinese "teapot" refineries, and even a land blockade.

Background: The campaign follows the recent US-Iran war and marks an escalation beyond conventional sanctions into secondary measures aimed at third countries, with Pakistan's army chief Munir mediating and Oman also shuttling between the parties.

Why it matters: Enforcing secondary sanctions against China, India and Russia would test Trump's detente with Beijing and could either break Iran's oil revenue or fracture the coalition needed to sustain pressure. It is the central determinant of whether the Iran conflict de-escalates or widens economically.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post amplifies collapse narratives (Trump's "completely collapsing," a laid-off worker's suicide attempt as proof of crisis) and frames it as an "endgame"; Al Jazeera reframes it as an "economic D-Day" that risks blowback from Beijing and foregrounds Iran's retaliation threat; the Guardian centres the credibility question — whether India, China and Russia actually comply — and quotes Iran calling it desperate while conceding domestic hardship; BBC reports the Treasury claim largely at face value.

2. Mossad chief, Syria FM hold positive de-escalation talks after Israeli airbase strike, source says

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: Mossad chief and Syrian Foreign Minister Asaad al-Shaibani held what a source described as positive de-escalation talks, days after an Israeli strike on a Syrian airbase reportedly intended to block a Turkish military presence at Abu al-Duhur. Shaibani publicly denied Turkey planned a base there, said the site was being restored for the Syrian Air Force, and told Reuters Israel has a "historic opportunity" for diplomacy while insisting Damascus's priority is halting Israeli strikes. US envoy Tom Barrack's apparent endorsement of the Turkish-Syrian account, and his subsequent reversal on Golan Heights comments, has strained his standing in Jerusalem.

Background: Since Assad's fall, Ahmed al-Sharaa's government has courted Turkey while Israel strikes what it calls Iranian weapons transfers to Hezbollah — pitting two US allies, Israel and Turkey, against each other over Syria's military future.

Why it matters: A US-brokered Israel-Syria security arrangement would be the most significant regional realignment since Assad's ouster, but a Turkey-Israel collision in Syria could open a new front just as the Iran file is escalating.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post carries the argument internally: some coverage presents the strikes as necessary interdiction of Iranian smuggling, while other reporting cites IDF sources saying no recent smuggling spike was detected and quotes critics accusing Netanyahu of manufacturing tension with Syria and Turkey for electoral gain. Syrian and Turkish accounts, relayed via Reuters and endorsed by the US envoy, deny the Turkish-base premise entirely.

3. Strait of Hormuz traffic plunges as Iran, US blockades restrict energy shipments

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: Vessel traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has fallen sharply — 89 ships exited and 103 entered over a seven-day window per AIS data — as competing Iranian and US restrictions squeeze energy shipments. Iran is simultaneously advancing a plan to charge transit fees on vessels, which the Jerusalem Post notes would violate UNCLOS Articles 38 and 44 barring tolls or impediment. Oman's foreign minister is travelling to Tehran and Pakistan's army chief arrived Monday to continue mediation specifically on the strait.

Background: Roughly a fifth of global oil normally transits Hormuz; Iran has long threatened closure as its ultimate deterrent, and the current squeeze follows the war and the US blockade effort.

Why it matters: A sustained throughput collapse or a toll regime would globalise the Iran confrontation through energy prices and freight insurance, and turns Omani and Pakistani mediation into the most consequential diplomatic track in the region.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post frames Iran's fee plan as a straightforward breach of international maritime law and attributes the disruption to Iranian action, while also reporting a senior Iranian official's boast of selling 70 million barrels and earning billions despite the blockade; Al Jazeera and the Guardian situate the same restrictions primarily as consequences of US pressure rather than Iranian obstruction.
