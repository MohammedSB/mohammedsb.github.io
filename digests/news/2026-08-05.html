1. US and Qatar report progress on Iran ceasefire and reopening Hormuz strait

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: US, Qatari and Omani officials all reported progress toward a deal that would restore a ceasefire in the US-Iran war and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, with ships entering the Gulf via an Iranian-controlled route and exiting through Omani waters. Trump said the strait would open "very soon" or Iran would be "hit very hard," while Iran's foreign ministry denied any direct talks with Washington and Qatar said no direct meetings are planned. Oil fell about 2% on the optimism, but the strait stayed closed to most traffic — only six to eight vessels transited, against a normal ~130.

Background: The strait has been shut amid a US-Iran war featuring months of American strikes on Iranian shipping-threat capabilities and Iranian attacks on US bases and commercial vessels, choking roughly a fifth of global oil flows.

Why it matters: A Hormuz deal would be the first concrete off-ramp from a war that has disrupted global energy markets and could open the door to renewed nuclear talks; failure invites another round of US strikes.

Different views: Al Jazeera and the Guardian frame it as a mediated diplomatic breakthrough toward peace talks, while the Jerusalem Post emphasizes that Iran would gain control over waters that were previously international — casting the deal as rewarding Tehran's coercion — and stresses Trump's claim that Iran "lied" about negotiating; Iranian officials publicly deny talks exist at all, which Western outlets read as face-saving for hardliners.

2. IDF begins precise strikes against Hezbollah in southern Lebanon after issuing evacuation warning

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: The IDF launched what it called "precise" or "targeted" strikes in southern Lebanon, saying they responded to Hezbollah breaching the ceasefire, and issued an evacuation warning for civilians — including the village of Mansouri — for the first time since June. Israel also said it destroyed Hezbollah tunnel systems and that more remain. The strikes came as Israeli and Lebanese delegations met for a second day in Rome, where they remained split over disarming Hezbollah and an Israeli withdrawal from the south, and over which countries would verify that areas the Lebanese army enters are Hezbollah-free.

Background: The October ceasefire left Hezbollah's disarmament and Israel's presence in south Lebanon unresolved, with US-brokered Rome talks the main channel since a previous round on 15 July.

Why it matters: Renewed strikes and civilian evacuation orders during active negotiations show how thin the Lebanon ceasefire is, and the verification dispute is the pivot on which any Israeli withdrawal now turns.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post leads on Israeli precision, prior warnings and destroyed tunnel infrastructure, framing the strikes as ceasefire enforcement and the Rome talks as "positive"; Al Jazeera foregrounds the deadlock and Israel's continued occupation of the south, and the Guardian's live coverage pairs the strikes with evacuation orders for named villages, keeping the civilian impact in frame.

3. Jerusalem’s most important Muslim religious site facing ‘imminent’ threat of Israeli takeover, says Jordan

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Jordan said the al-Aqsa mosque compound faces an "imminent" threat of Israeli takeover and convened an emergency meeting of foreign ministers from across the Arab and Islamic world, warning such a move could "trigger a religious conflict." Amman, as custodian of the site, is seeking collective diplomatic pressure to head it off.

Background: Jordan has held the Hashemite custodianship of Jerusalem's Muslim holy sites since 1924, under a status quo repeatedly strained by Israeli far-right ministers pushing for Jewish prayer and control at the compound.

Why it matters: Al-Aqsa is the region's most combustible flashpoint; a change to the status quo could ignite escalation far beyond Jerusalem and jeopardize Israel's peace treaty with Jordan at a moment when regional diplomacy is already overstretched.

Different views: The Guardian gives Jordan's warning prominence and treats the takeover threat as credible and imminent; Israeli coverage largely omits or downplays the alarm, and the parallel Al Jazeera story on protests against Smotrich-linked settler funding reflects a pro-Palestinian framing in which Israeli government ministers are the driving force behind such encroachment.
