1. Netanyahu rejects US-led 15-point Gaza peace plan in rare public break with Trump

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu publicly rejected the US-backed 15-point peace plan for Gaza, saying the IDF "will not carry out any withdrawal until Hamas is genuinely disarmed." He framed the statement as a response to a "wave of rumours about Israeli weakness and Israeli withdrawals," and rebuked US pressure to end wars without Israel's buy-in — a rare open break with Donald Trump.

Background: Trump has been pushing to wind down both the Gaza war and the confrontation with Iran, hailing the 15-point framework as a path to ending the fighting. Netanyahu remains dependent on far-right coalition partners such as Itamar Ben-Gvir who oppose any withdrawal or negotiated end to the war.

Why it matters: It is the most visible US-Israel rift of the war and effectively stalls the leading diplomatic track for ending it, prolonging the fighting and the humanitarian catastrophe in Gaza. It also tests whether Washington will use leverage on an Israeli government that is now openly defying it.

Different views: The BBC reports it flatly as a rejection hinging on Hamas disarmament, while the Guardian foregrounds the "rare public break with Trump" and Netanyahu's rebuke of American pressure; Jerusalem Post coverage sidesteps the rift and instead questions Trump's reliability, with Israeli analysts warning he "could reverse policy once again" and reporting that regional states plan to sign deals with Israel only to violate them later. Al Jazeera's Gaza coverage centres the civilian and journalist death toll rather than the diplomatic mechanics, implicitly treating Israeli refusal to withdraw as continuation of a campaign against Palestinians.

2. Trump willing to end war, declare victory on Iran with no nuclear deal, officials say - report

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: The Wall Street Journal reported that Trump has privately floated ending the war with Iran and declaring victory without securing a nuclear deal, with Iran's reopening of the Strait of Hormuz to international shipping as the key condition. Tehran says an Oman-mediated agreement is in its "final stages" but insists Washington must move first, and IRGC figure Mohammed Bagher Zolghadr publicly set out six conditions for the US to "correct its behavior."

Background: The Strait of Hormuz — which carried about a fifth of global oil and LNG shipments before the war — has been closed or badly disrupted since the US-Israel war on Iran began, stranding up to 20,000 seafarers in the Gulf. Trump has simultaneously shifted from military escalation back to economic pressure on Iran's economy.

Why it matters: The terms of any Hormuz deal will determine global energy flows, Gulf food and fuel supply, and whether the war ends with Iran's nuclear program untouched. It would also mark Tehran converting a chokepoint into durable strategic leverage over Washington.

Different views: Jerusalem Post coverage, citing INSS and NYT-sourced analysts, casts Iran as unlikely ever to willingly surrender Hormuz and its demands as recycled terms already conceded in an earlier MoU — framing a deal as an American capitulation that would deny Israel a strategic win. Al Jazeera presents Trump's pivot to economic pressure as tacit acknowledgement that military force failed, while Iranian officials quoted in Israeli media frame the standoff as the US needing to correct its own behaviour.

3. Iran-aligned Houthis claim they attacked oil refinery in Saudi Arabia

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthis claimed a drone attack on Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery in southwestern Saudi Arabia; the Saudi Energy Ministry said firefighters extinguished the resulting fire. The strike came two days after Riyadh signed a defence pact — the "Mecca Agreement" — with Turkey and Pakistan, and as Tehran issued hard demands for reopening the Strait of Hormuz.

Background: Saudi Arabia has absorbed repeated attacks from Iran, the Houthis and Iraqi militias during the US-Israel war on Iran without retaliating, and signed the trilateral pact amid eroding trust in Washington's security guarantees. Pakistan describes the agreement as "purely defensive" and open to other members.

Why it matters: An attack on Saudi energy infrastructure days after the pact suggests the new alignment has no deterrent effect yet, and raises the risk of the war spreading to Gulf oil and gas facilities already strained by the Hormuz shipping crisis.

Different views: The Guardian links the strike to Tehran's Hormuz demands and reads the pact as Saudi hedging against an unreliable US; the Jerusalem Post quotes experts calling the pact a "paper tiger" that Iran views as a major win, and warns it complicates Israeli normalisation hopes while pushing Israel closer to India, Greece and Cyprus. Iran's foreign ministry, quoted in Israeli media, welcomes the pact as evidence regional states are relying on their own capabilities rather than outside powers — a framing Riyadh's partners echo by stressing it targets no one.
