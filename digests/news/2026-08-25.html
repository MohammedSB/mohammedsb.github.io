1. US Treasury announces 'Operation Economic Outcast' on Iran following warning of 'economic D-Day'

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: The US Treasury launched "Operation Economic Outcast," a sweeping sanctions campaign that Secretary Scott Bessent billed as an "economic D-Day" and the "single greatest financial offensive ever marshalled against an adversary," targeting Iranian petroleum and petrochemical exports, military-linked entities and, critically, any third country or company that keeps trading with Tehran. The State Department added parallel designations and a $10m Rewards for Justice bounty on senior IRGC figures. Tehran said it is "fully prepared" to counter the measures and vowed to retaliate against any state that cooperates, while Beijing called the secondary-sanctions threat illegal and promised "all necessary measures" to defend its interests.

Background: The campaign follows the recent US-Israeli military confrontation with Iran and represents a shift to economic strangulation after force failed to produce Iranian capitulation. Iran has survived multiple sanctions waves since 2018, largely by selling discounted oil to China.

Why it matters: Whether the campaign works hinges almost entirely on whether Washington is willing to sanction major Chinese banks and refiners — a step that would collide with Trump-Xi trade diplomacy and could fracture global energy markets. It is the central test of whether US economic coercion can now substitute for war against a state adversary.

Different views: Jerusalem Post frames it as an overdue "endgame" and "economic onslaught" that will sever Tehran's lifelines, cataloguing the measures approvingly; BBC leads with the skeptical question of whether sanctions can topple a regime that has outlasted them before; the Guardian stresses that Washington is trying to achieve economically what it failed to achieve militarily and doubts US willingness to confront China; Al Jazeera reframes the story around the costs to third parties, noting the US sanctions Chinese firms but spares the big banks.

2. US removes Syria from list of state sponsors of terrorism

BBC Middle East · UK · mainstream

What happened: The State Department formally removed Syria from the US list of State Sponsors of Terrorism after the previously announced decision cleared congressional review, completing Washington's embrace of President Ahmed al-Sharaa, a former al-Qaeda-linked militant commander. The move lands as Damascus announces a "new phase" in accounting for the Assad regime's chemical weapons legacy and as a Syrian court sentenced Assad-era grand mufti Ahmed Hassoun to life in prison for inciting civil war and complicity in killings.

Background: Syria had been on the list since 1979. Al-Sharaa's forces toppled Bashar al-Assad and the new government has spent the past period courting Western recognition and sanctions relief.

Why it matters: Delisting unlocks investment, banking access and reconstruction financing that Syria cannot function without, and locks the US into a bet that al-Sharaa's government will consolidate rather than fragment. It also raises the stakes of the parallel Israeli-Turkish contest over Syrian territory.

Different views: BBC foregrounds al-Sharaa's al-Qaeda past as the uncomfortable core of the story; the Jerusalem Post reports the delisting more procedurally while its analysis pieces treat post-Assad Syria mainly as a security file — Israeli strikes, night raids, weapons smuggling and an accountability test on chemical weapons — with Israeli officials themselves split over whether Netanyahu is manufacturing tension with Damascus and Ankara for electoral reasons.

3. Strait of Hormuz traffic plunges as Iran, US blockades restrict energy shipments

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli · right-leaning

What happened: Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has collapsed, with AIS data showing only 89 vessels exiting and 103 entering over a seven-day period as Iranian threats and attacks on shipping combine with US interdiction to shut down a waterway that normally carries roughly a fifth of the world's energy. Tehran is simultaneously advancing a plan to charge transit fees on vessels using the strait — a move that would breach UNCLOS Articles 38 and 44 — while Oman's foreign minister travels to Tehran to continue negotiations over the passage.

Background: The Strait of Hormuz is the chokepoint for Gulf oil and LNG exports; Iran has long threatened to close it but has never sustained a shutdown of this scale. Oman is Tehran's habitual back-channel to Washington.

Why it matters: A sustained Hormuz disruption is Iran's most potent form of escalation short of war, and it hurts Gulf Arab producers and Asian importers more than it hurts the US — turning them, not Washington, into the constituency pressing Tehran for a deal.

Different views: The Jerusalem Post frames the fee plan as a legal violation and catalogues Hormuz disruption as an Iranian escalation option; Al Jazeera's coverage of the same standoff centres instead on which trading partners the US would have to target to isolate Iran, treating the pressure campaign rather than Iranian action as the driver.
