1. Trump threatens to declare strait of Hormuz ‘territory of the United States’

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Donald Trump said in a speech on Long Island that he would "pretty soon" declare the Strait of Hormuz "a territory of the United States," as his administration struggles to bring the war with Iran to a conclusion. Iran's Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi responded that Iran "neither fears threats nor cowers before displays of power," while the head of Iran's Basij paramilitary insisted the waterway is "under Iran's control and management." US Treasury Secretary Bessent separately promised "economic isolation like the world has never seen before" plus a continued blockade of the strait.

Background: The Strait of Hormuz is the chokepoint for roughly a fifth of global oil shipments and has become the central theatre of the ongoing US-Iran war, with shipping traffic already falling sharply.

Why it matters: A US claim of sovereignty over an international waterway bordered by Iran and Oman would be legally unprecedented and could lock Washington into an open-ended naval commitment, while the blockade and sanctions threat keeps global energy markets and Gulf states exposed to escalation.

Different views: The Guardian foregrounds the ambiguity — noting it is unclear whether the remark was serious policy or rhetoric, and framing it against a war Washington cannot end; the Jerusalem Post presents it as a victory statement following Iran's defeat and pairs it with Iranian defiance, while Iranian officials quoted across outlets treat it as empty bluster over a strait they claim to control.

2. Israeli strikes on south Lebanon kill nine in worst toll since June

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Two Israeli airstrikes hit homes in the southern Lebanese villages of Ansar and Deir al-Zahrani, killing nine people including three children and wounding 11 — the deadliest day in Lebanon since the June agreements curtailed Israel-Hezbollah hostilities. Rescue teams searched the rubble for survivors. Lebanese President Joseph Aoun and Prime Minister Nawaf Salam condemned the strikes as a violation of the framework agreement and demanded they stop.

Background: June 2026 understandings sharply reduced fighting between Israel and Hezbollah, but Israel has continued strikes it says target Hezbollah's rebuilding of military infrastructure, and Washington has been pressing for Hezbollah's disarmament.

Why it matters: The heaviest casualty toll since the truce raises the risk that the Lebanon front reopens, and it puts Lebanon's government in the position of either confronting Hezbollah or absorbing continued Israeli strikes.

Different views: Al Jazeera leads with the family home and civilian dead, filming the aftermath and describing intensifying attacks on Lebanon; the Guardian reports the civilian toll and truce breakdown in neutral terms; the Jerusalem Post frames the same strikes as the IDF targeting Hezbollah infrastructure and covers the Lebanese reaction primarily as Aoun's condemnation rather than as established fact about who was hit.

3. Israeli military plans to transfer law enforcement in occupied West Bank to police

The Guardian · UK · left-leaning

What happened: Israeli Defence Minister Israel Katz announced a plan to transfer law enforcement powers over Israelis in the occupied West Bank from the military to the Israel Police. Palestine's vice-president called it a "flagrant violation of international law" and a step toward full annexation. The move lands amid a settler siege of the West Bank town of Kusra, where extremist settlers have encircled Palestinian homes for nearly a week, with US officials reportedly pressing Netanyahu to condemn it and activists attempting to deliver aid to trapped families.

Background: Since 1967 the West Bank has been under Israeli military administration, the legal architecture that international bodies treat as evidence of occupation rather than sovereignty; shifting civilian policing to Israeli police applies domestic Israeli governance to the territory.

Why it matters: Replacing military jurisdiction with civilian policing is the practical machinery of de facto annexation, and doing so while settler violence goes unchecked deepens the two-tier legal system Palestinians and rights groups describe as apartheid.

Different views: The Guardian centres the annexation charge and Palestinian legal objections; the Jerusalem Post covers the linked settler siege mainly through the lens of American pressure on Netanyahu and Israeli political discomfort, treating settler violence as a diplomatic problem; Al Jazeera and the BBC document the ground-level effects — raids on a West Bank wedding hall, families besieged and cut off from aid.
