1. Iran school bombing: grounds to believe US was behind atrocity, UN finds

The Guardian · UK

What happened: A UN fact-finding mission concluded there are reasonable grounds to believe US forces carried out February's strikes on a primary school in Minab and a sports complex in Lamerd, killing roughly 120 children among more than 150 civilians, and that these attacks amount to war crimes. The same UN process also found Iranian authorities committed crimes against humanity in suppressing protests. Washington has not acknowledged the strikes and the White House has rejected the findings.

Background: The strikes occurred during the ongoing US-Iran war, in which the US has deployed more than 50,000 troops to the region and Iran has downed US drones over the Strait of Hormuz.

Why it matters: A formal UN war-crimes determination against the US changes the legal and diplomatic terrain of the Iran war, giving allies pressed to join the campaign — such as South Korea — a public reason to stay out.

Different views: One version leads on the child death toll and the legal finding itself, treating the UN mission as authoritative and the US denial as a response; another pairs the finding with the parallel accusation against Iran over the protest crackdown, so the headline reads as symmetrical culpability rather than a US atrocity; a third foregrounds what it means that Washington has never admitted carrying out the strikes at all.

2. Thursday briefing: Yemen’s war continues to threaten oil supplies– but what about its people?

The Guardian · UK

What happened: Houthi forces advanced rapidly along Yemen's Red Sea coastal plain and toward Taiz, seizing positions at the Bab al-Mandeb strait. Over 112,000–125,000 people have been displaced, with thousands crossing to Djibouti, and Saudi Arabia intercepted a Houthi drone that killed at least one person on its territory while a Saudi F-15 was shot down over Yemen.

Background: The Houthis, built up over a decade with Iranian weapons and components, now control a chokepoint through which a large share of global oil and container traffic passes; Riyadh is reportedly probing Oman-mediated ceasefire contacts while Pakistan pledges to defend Saudi territory under the new Mecca joint defence pact.

Why it matters: Control of Bab al-Mandeb hands a non-state actor leverage over global energy and shipping at the same moment the US is stretched by the Iran war, and it reopens a humanitarian emergency in the world's poorest Arab state.

Different views: Coverage splits on what the advance is a story about: one strand centres the 125,000 displaced Yemenis and asks who is reporting on them rather than on tanker rates; another frames it as a capability and threat story, counting Houthi fighters, Iranian-supplied components and the disruption to shipping into Eilat; a third reads US restraint as a stretched Washington quietly accepting Houthi assurances on shipping rather than fighting a second front.

3. US approves possible sale of F-35 fighter jets to Saudi Arabia

The Jerusalem Post · Israeli

What happened: The US approved a possible sale of F-35 fighter jets to Saudi Arabia, ending years of Saudi lobbying for the aircraft. The approval lands while Saudi Arabia is fighting the Houthis, has lost an F-15 over Yemen and is absorbing drone strikes on its own territory.

Background: The F-35 has been sold in the region only to Israel, whose qualitative military edge US law is meant to preserve; the deal was first floated in late 2025.

Why it matters: Selling the aircraft to Riyadh would erode the technological gap that has underpinned Israel's regional posture and signals how far Washington will go to keep Saudi Arabia inside its camp during the Iran and Yemen wars.

Different views: One account frames the controversy around technology transfer, end-use and human-rights concerns generally; another frames it almost entirely as a question of whether Israel's military advantage survives, treating that edge as the natural benchmark against which the sale must be judged.
